# AI Models and Copyright Law: The Ongoing Legal Battle

---

## The Core Issue: AI Training vs Copyright

The AI models powering ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are trained on vast datasets containing hundreds of millions of books, online articles, academic papers, and virtually everything on the internet. Most authors have contributed to these datasets without their knowledge or consent, raising a fundamental question: is this legal?

---

## The Anthropic Ruling: $1.5B Settlement and Fair Use

Last year, Judge William Alsup ordered Anthropic to pay a $1.5 billion copyright settlement to writers whose works trained the company's AI models. While this appeared to be a victory for authors, Alsup actually ruled that Anthropic's AI training itself was lawful. The penalty was for pirating books from illegal online shadow libraries.

"Like any reader aspiring to be a writer, Anthropic's LLMs trained upon works not to race ahead and replicate or supplant them -- but to turn a hard corner and create something different," the judge wrote, comparing LLM training to a writer studying literature.

However, legal experts note the ruling heavily favors AI companies. With Anthropic projecting $200 billion in annual revenue by 2028, a $1.5 billion fine is a minor cost of doing business.

---

## Fair Use Doctrine: The Key Battleground

Copyright law hasn't been updated since 1976, forcing courts to apply 50-year-old guidelines to AI-related questions that will shape the industry's future.

These cases hinge on "fair use" -- whether AI training constitutes transformative use sufficient to be legally permissible. Fair use protects commentary, criticism, education, and parody. Courts examine the purpose and nature of the work, the amount used, and the impact on the market.

The Thomson Reuters vs Ross Intelligence case illustrates this tension. Ross trained on Reuters content to build a competing AI legal platform. The court ruled this was NOT fair use because Ross's platform directly competed with Thomson Reuters -- it lacked the "different character" required for transformative use.

---

## The Broader Implications

Most AI companies remain entangled in ongoing litigation. The initial wave of rulings is influential, but outcomes remain uncertain as different courts reach different conclusions.

A related issue: works that are 100% AI-generated cannot be copyrighted, per the Thaler v. Perlmutter ruling -- creating additional legal complications. The legal framework continues to evolve as courts grapple with fundamental questions about creativity, authorship, and machine learning.

As attorney Cathy Gellis noted, "If you write your novel in Word and run spell check, we don't say Word owns your novel. AI is forcing us to rethink these foundational concepts."
