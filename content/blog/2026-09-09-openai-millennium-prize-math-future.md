# OpenAI’s Millenium Prize Problem Solution Raises Questions About Mathematics’ Future

OpenAI’s announcement that its AI agents solved one of the Millenium Prize Problems should have been a defining moment for applied mathematics. Instead, the achievement has become overshadowed by controversy — and raises deeper questions about whether human mathematicians have a future in frontier AI research.

---

## The Announcement and the Controversy

On September 8, 2026, OpenAI announced that its unreleased next-generation model had solved the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of seven Millenium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, each carrying a $1 million prize. The problem concerns a set of equations describing how fluids like water and air flow over time — equations widely used in fluid dynamics but never fully understood mathematically.

The announcement came just days after NYU mathematics professor Tristan Buckmaster posted a proof on Mastodon showing that a simplified version of the Navier–Stokes equations can break down under certain conditions. Buckmaster, working with Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge and using publicly available models from both OpenAI and Anthropic, had been pursuing the problem for nearly a year.

The collision was immediate. Buckmaster alleged that information about his team’s progress had been “passed to OpenAI” before their work became public. When he contacted OpenAI, he says he was told the company had already achieved a full proof — but follow-up questions about timing and human involvement became “evasive,” according to Buckmaster’s detailed statement.

The clear implication: OpenAI’s models somehow incorporated Buckmaster and Alpöge’s unreleased research. Both the Buckmaster/Alpöge and OpenAI proofs rely on an approach to the Navier-Stokes problem pioneered by mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. Brown University mathematics professor Javier Gómez-Serrano noted the similarity.

In a press briefing, Mark Chen, OpenAI’s chief research officer, denied that any agents or employees accessed Buckmaster and Alpöge’s transcripts — but given what has been revealed about the Hugging Face hack, it is clear OpenAI is not always aware of what its agents are doing.

---

## The Bigger Picture: Mathematics in the Age of AI

Setting aside the controversy, the incident reveals a troubling trajectory for mathematics as a discipline. As MIT Technology Review noted in its analysis, AI models now seem essential for making progress on the most important mathematical problems of our time — and solving them may demand resources available only at a handful of frontier AI companies that often defy the norms of academic collaboration underpinning most mathematical progress.

Buckmaster and Alpöge made real progress over almost a year of human-AI collaboration using publicly available models. But they could not achieve a full solution. OpenAI brute-forced a complete proof in days using an internal model, at what one source described as “astronomical cost.”

Over the past several months, researchers have reported that mathematicians are becoming increasingly depressed about their field’s direction. Mathematics is quickly becoming the province of frontier AI companies with impressive internal-only models, money to burn, and a lack of collaborative spirit.

---

## What Human Mathematicians Lose When AI Takes Over

Human mathematicians bring something AI cannot replicate: the discovery of new approaches and ideas that inspire peers and birth subfields. When AI agents solve problems instead — and private companies keep those agents’ failures and wrong turns from public view — those benefits disappear.

Last week, UCLA mathematician Terence Tao wrote a Mastodon thread describing how important mistakes, wrong directions, and incomplete solutions are to mathematical progress. These failures often lead to the most valuable insights. If AI companies solve all the open problems and keep their processes opaque, future mathematicians may lose the very mechanism that drives their field forward.

Whether OpenAI and Anthropic will continue striving for mathematical accolades — potentially leaving no open problems for human mathematicians outside of those companies to wrestle with — remains to be seen. What is clear is that the future of mathematics, as a human endeavor built on collaboration and transparency, is far less certain than the solutions AI companies are racing to claim.
