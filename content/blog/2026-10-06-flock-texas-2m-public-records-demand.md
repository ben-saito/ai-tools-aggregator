# Fort Worth Suburb Demands $2.3M for Flock License Plate Reader Public Records

The city of North Richland Hills, a Fort Worth suburb in Texas, has responded to a public records request for data on its Flock Safety license plate readers with a $2.3 million fee estimate—claims that privacy advocates say are designed to discourage oversight rather than reflect actual costs.

---

## The Records Request

The Texas Privacy Coalition, filing through representative Phil Mynona, submitted a public records request seeking communications, error logs, and effectiveness data for the Flock Safety automated license plate reader (ALPR) system used by local police. The request covered approximately one terabyte of data.

City officials claimed that searching and compiling the records would require roughly 14 years of labor at an hourly rate of $15, producing the $2.3 million estimate. Texas law permits agencies to charge reasonable fees for public records searches, but the magnitude of this estimate has drawn scrutiny as a potential de facto denial of access.

Privacy advocates argue that the fee structure is engineered to discourage public oversight rather than recover costs—a pattern they say has become more common as ALPR technology faces increasing legal and regulatory challenges.

## Flock Safety Under Scrutiny

Flock Safety's license plate readers continuously scan passing vehicles, capturing license plates, timestamps, and vehicle descriptions. The company has become a focal point of privacy debates as its cameras have proliferated across American communities, with courts in other jurisdictions describing the technology as "indiscriminate mass surveillance."

The Texas Privacy Coalition has been filing similar public records requests across multiple jurisdictions, arguing that the cumulative surveillance data warrants public scrutiny. The group's requests have consistently encountered high fee estimates or incomplete responses.

Flock Safety has faced a wave of legal and regulatory challenges including court rulings questioning the legality of the technology, several jurisdictions moving to ban or restrict the cameras, and recent corporate restructuring attributed in part to the mounting backlash against mass surveillance systems.
