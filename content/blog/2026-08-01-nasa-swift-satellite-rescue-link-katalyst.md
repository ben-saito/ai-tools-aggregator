# NASA Swift Observatory Rescue: Katalyst's Link Satellite Racing Against Time

The Swift gamma-ray burst observatory, a NASA mission that has been operating since 2004, faces an unprecedented rescue attempt after its orbit decayed faster than expected. Katalyst Space Technologies, a satellite servicing startup, launched the Link satellite on July 3 to rendezvous with and boost Swift's orbit under a $30 million NASA contract — the first time NASA has contracted commercial services to rescue one of its satellites.

---

## A Race Against Atmospheric Drag

Swift's orbit has been decaying due to atmospheric drag, and the window for a successful rescue is closing fast. Katalyst's ground team, working near Denver, discovered the spacecraft had entered an unintended spin during one of the communication passes. The Link satellite's other systems — including its power supply, three xenon-fueled electric thrusters, and rendezvous hardware — remained healthy.

The ground team immediately began recovery operations, using the plasma engines to gradually slow the spin by thrusting in the opposite direction of rotation. "We've been able to use the thrusters to point in the opposite direction of the rotation rate and affect the attitude control that way, which has been highly effective," said Ghonhee Lee, CEO of Katalyst, in an interview with Ars Technica.

---

## First Commercial Satellite Servicing of a NASA Asset

This mission represents a milestone in NASA's approach to satellite servicing. Rather than building and operating dedicated servicing spacecraft, NASA contracted Katalyst to perform the rescue, marking the first commercial mission to service an active NASA asset. The agency gave Katalyst less than a year to put the entire mission together — an unusually tight timeline for a satellite servicing operation.

Link's electric propulsion system, while efficient, produces low thrust, meaning recovery operations take time to accumulate the necessary orbital impulse. The spacecraft's xenon thrusters are primarily designed for orbit-raising but can vector their thrust for attitude control via a two-axis gimbal.

---

## Technical Challenges and Timeline

Katalyst's team is working around the clock to stabilize Link before Swift's orbit decays beyond the point of no return. The spacecraft must complete the rendezvous and capture before aerodynamic drag pulls Swift too low. Once captured, Link will use its electric thrusters to boost Swift back into a stable orbit, extending the observatory's operational life.

The stakes are high: Swift has been a cornerstone of gamma-ray burst research for over two decades, and its loss would create a significant gap in NASA's ability to detect and study these energetic cosmic events.
