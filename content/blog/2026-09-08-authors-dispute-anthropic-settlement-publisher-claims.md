# Authors Push Back as Publishers Claim Share of Anthropic's $1.5B Copyright Settlement

Writers whose works were included in Anthropic's $1.5 billion copyright settlement are pushing back after receiving notifications that publishers and literary agents are making claims on their payment shares. The dispute highlights ongoing tensions around how settlement funds are distributed when AI companies train on copyrighted material.

---

## The Settlement Background

Anthropic settled a copyright class action lawsuit last year, after a court ruled that training AI models on copyrighted material falls under fair use doctrine, but actively pirating that material does not. The settlement received final approval in July 2026, clearing the way for payments to proceed.

Under the settlement terms, authors of nearly 500,000 titles are eligible for $3,000 per pirated work. The distribution follows a specific formula: if a book remains in print with a traditional publisher, the payment is split 50-50 between author and publisher. For self-published works, or titles where publishers have reverted rights by allowing the book to go out of print, the author receives the full amount.

---

## Publishers and Agents Claiming Author Shares

Despite the clear terms, authors have taken to social media to report that publishers are aggressively claiming portions of their settlements. Mystery and thriller author April Henry publicly questioned HarperCollins's approach, noting that the publisher had claimed a share of payments that, under the settlement terms, should go entirely to the author.

The dispute centers on works where authors believed they held full rights — self-published titles and books that have fallen out of print. In these cases, the settlement explicitly awards the full $3,000 to the author. Publishers and agents, however, appear to be interpreting their existing contracts broadly in an attempt to capture portions of these payments as well.

---

## Implications for AI Copyright Battles

The controversy reflects the broader complexity of the AI copyright landscape. While the Anthropic settlement established a framework for compensating authors whose works were used in AI training, the implementation reveals gaps and ambiguities in how existing publishing contracts interact with AI-era licensing deals.

Authors and publishing industry observers are watching closely to see how the dispute resolves, as it could set precedent for how future AI training compensation is distributed across the publishing ecosystem.
