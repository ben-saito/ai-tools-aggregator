# We Can't Help Treating AI Like It's Human. But Should We?

---

A strange thing happens when people encounter a robot — even one they know is just a machine. At MIT's CSAIL lab, a visitor found himself shaking the hand of a Unitree humanoid robot and saying "Hello, sir, it's nice to meet you." He knew it wasn't sentient. He shook its hand anyway.

This impulse to treat AI systems like people runs deeper than politeness, according to new research and a forthcoming book from MIT's Dr. Sherry Turkle. Studies show roughly 70% of people are polite to AI chatbots, and users are significantly more likely to say "please" and "thank you" once a conversation progresses past the first exchange.

## The Psychology of Artificial Intimacy

Turkle's new book, "Artificial Intimacy," charts how humans have related to computers from the first home computers to modern large language models. Her central finding: we are neurologically wired to care for things that appear to care for us, and this wiring gets triggered even by primitive AI systems.

"People tend to categorize whether this is the right use or wrong use, but I don't think that is a clear boundary," said Dr. Pat Pataranutaporn, who runs the Cyborg Psychology research group at MIT Media Lab. "The question is, who are we becoming when we talk to [AI]?"

The stakes of this question grow larger as LLMs infiltrate Google searches, customer service interactions, and workplace tools. The reflexive politeness that feels harmless with a $50,000 robot becomes more consequential when applied to AI systems making hiring decisions, medical diagnoses, or legal determinations.

## When Anthropomorphism Becomes a Liability

The tendency to humanize AI creates specific risks beyond wasted social energy. People increasingly ascribe legitimacy to AI outputs they wouldn't accept from a human — a phenomenon researchers call "automation complacency." If an AI system presents information confidently, users often accept it uncritically, even when the system's reasoning would be obviously flawed if attributed to a person.

This dynamic also cuts the other direction: some users become inappropriately hostile to AI systems, treating algorithmic errors as moral failings rather than technical problems to be fixed.

As AI systems become more sophisticated and more deeply integrated into daily life, the question of how to relate to them may require more deliberate thought. The instinctive response — treat it like a person — may not always serve us well.
