Rivian recently rolled out an AI assistant that lets drivers and passengers interact with the company's electric vehicles just by talking to them. But the carmaker's founder and CEO, RJ Scaringe, said at TechCrunch Disrupt this week that, in the future, Rivian's cars will be able to run AI agents made by other companies like Anthropic. "That's where it becomes magical," Scaringe said on stage.

Scaringe didn't say when Rivian would implement this, and the company declined to offer more details, pointing instead to the "AI & Autonomy Day" event it's hosting in December. But it's not hard to imagine how Rivian might integrate something like Claude, OpenAI's Dots or Meta's Muse assistant into its software.

The company has developed its in-car software from the ground up, instead of leaning on Apple CarPlay or Android Auto, and built a lot of its own applications, like navigation (though it has worked to integrate popular in-car apps like Apple Music). Rivian says it has created its own in-house agentic framework that understands what action a person wants to take when they talk to the vehicle, and routes it to the right part of the car's software.

Most of the time, that involves what you'd expect: asking the car to turn down the temperature, activate the seat warmers, or summarize the news. "Think of it as like a traffic light. So if you ask the vehicle something, it decides what model it's going to use. It can use a local model that's running on the edge and the vehicle. If it's a more complex question, it can go up to the cloud. And then even for that, we have different models that we use," Scaringe said.

As of May of this year, the agent could access external apps like Google Calendar. And Scaringe said at Disrupt the next step would be to add products from the frontier AI companies, though making them work in the car won't be easy. "There's a lot of plumbing behind the scenes we have to do to make sure that that works, and it's not just within Rivian. That's also a function of those providers having an agent offering that is relevant for that context," he said.

The vision extends beyond simple voice commands. Rivian's agents would handle complex, multi-step tasks like coordinating with a user's calendar to find charging stations along a route, or adjusting vehicle settings based on learned preferences. The in-vehicle AI framework could serve as a broker between multiple AI providers, selecting the right model for each specific task.

---

## Multi-Provider AI Integration in Vehicles

The key to Rivian's approach is a local agentic routing layer that can delegate to both edge models running on the vehicle's hardware and cloud-based AI services. For simple queries, a local model provides fast responses without requiring a network connection. More complex requests — like generating a personalized travel itinerary or analyzing dashcam footage — get routed to cloud models from Anthropic, OpenAI, or Meta.

This hybrid architecture addresses a core challenge of in-vehicle AI: maintaining responsiveness for basic commands while enabling sophisticated capabilities that require more compute. It also positions Rivian as a platform that can absorb advances from multiple AI providers rather than being locked into a single vendor.

Scaringe's December "AI & Autonomy Day" event is expected to provide more concrete details about Rivian's roadmap for third-party AI agent integration. The company has been building its own AI team over the past year, and job postings suggest it's particularly interested in engineers with experience in autonomous systems and foundation model development.

## Industry Context

Rivian's move toward open AI integration mirrors a broader trend in the automotive industry. Mercedes-Benz has partnered with Microsoft for in-car AI assistants, while General Motors has explored OpenAI integration through its OnStar system. Tesla, meanwhile, has taken a more closed approach, relying exclusively on its in-house AI systems for Autopilot and Full Self-Driving features.

The key differentiator for Rivian may be its willingness to serve as a neutral broker rather than picking a single AI partner. By building an agentic framework that can route requests to Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta, and others, Rivian avoids the risk of being left behind if one AI provider falls behind in capability.

The vehicle as an AI agent host represents a natural evolution of the car from a transportation tool to an intelligent companion. As AI assistants become more capable at reasoning, planning, and executing multi-step tasks, the car becomes another environment where agents can operate on behalf of their users — scheduling maintenance, coordinating with smart home devices, or navigating complex logistics like multi-modal journeys.

For now, Rivian's immediate focus remains on perfecting its built-in voice assistant, which handles requests like climate control, media playback, and navigation. The more ambitious vision of running third-party AI agents will require not just technical integration but also careful consideration of liability, safety validation, and user privacy.
