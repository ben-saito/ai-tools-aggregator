# Peer Review Overwhelmed: Can Academic Publishing Survive the AI Era?

The academic peer review system is straining under an unprecedented surge in research publications, raising questions about whether the cornerstone of scientific publishing can adapt to the AI era. Research papers indexed in Scopus and Web of Science are growing at 5.6 percent annually, with AI-assisted writing tools making it easier than ever to produce and submit manuscripts.

---

## The Bottleneck No One Expected

Peer review has long been the gold standard for validating scientific claims. Volunteer experts read submitted manuscripts, assess methodology and conclusions, and recommend whether work deserves publication. It is anonymous, unpaid, and increasingly unsustainable.

A telling anecdote from Ars Technica: health economist Cesare Semprini submitted a study on HPV vaccination policy effectiveness to a journal. The single assigned reviewer misunderstood the paper's central question — confusing policy effectiveness with vaccine efficacy — and the manuscript was rejected. With only one reviewer and no second opinion, a potentially valuable study was shelved.

This is not an isolated incident. Across disciplines, researchers report that reviews are taking longer, feedback quality is declining, and the system is showing its age.

---

## AI as Both Cause and Cure

The irony is that AI is implicated in creating the problem and may be necessary to solve it. Large language models can help researchers write papers faster, explore literature more thoroughly, and even suggest methodological improvements. Tools like Semantic Scholar's GPT-powered features and Connected Papers help authors navigate vast bodies of prior work.

But the same tools are flooding journals with submissions, overwhelming the human reviewers who must evaluate them. A reviewer cannot read 50 papers a week when each is twice as long and twice as dense as it would have been a decade ago.

Some journals are experimenting with AI-assisted review, using LLMs to flag methodological issues, check for statistical errors, or identify potential plagiarism. Others are exploring open peer review models where preprints receive public commentary before formal submission.

---

## What Comes Next

The scientific community is at an inflection point. The traditional peer review model was designed for a world where publication was slow, expensive, and limited by human typesetting capacity. None of those constraints apply today.

Whether the system evolves through incremental reform or fundamental restructuring, one thing is clear: the current model cannot absorb unlimited growth in research output without either collapsing under its own weight or becoming so superficial as to lose meaning.
