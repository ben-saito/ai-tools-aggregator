# MIT/Columbia Paper: Why AI Companies Cannot Easily Coordinate to Slow Down

When leading AI firms are racing against each other to build the most powerful systems, can they be persuaded to slow down collectively? A new paper from researchers at MIT and Columbia, titled "Racing to Ruin," applies game theory to this question and concludes that two variables are essential: transparency about technology development, and trust between competing firms.

---

## The Game Theory of AI Arms Racing

The paper models competition between two frontier AI firms as a game where each firm knows that scaling technology raises the hazard of an irreversible disaster that drives all firms' profits to zero. The disaster risk comes from developing the technology, not from deploying it — creating a classic prisoner's dilemma structure.

The key finding: when monitoring is sufficiently precise, every equilibrium stops in finite time. However, a new temptation immediately appears — each firm would like to be the second to stop, exiting only after confirming the rival has stopped first.

"Trust, but verify" becomes the operative framework: firms need both the willingness to slow down and the ability to verify that competitors are doing the same.

---

## The Paradox of Transparency

The paper reveals a counterintuitive property of transparency: it is double-edged. While transparency enables coordination, at intermediate trust levels, increased transparency can actually destroy early-stopping equilibria by making free-riding attractive — a firm might wait for confirmation that its rival stopped without committing to stop first.

---

## Implications for AI Governance

The researchers argue that if society hopes to slow or pause the development of powerful AI systems, it needs regimes for companies to share information about the state of their AI development, plus tools to verify that claimed slowdowns are genuine. This parallels historical arms control frameworks for nuclear weapons.
