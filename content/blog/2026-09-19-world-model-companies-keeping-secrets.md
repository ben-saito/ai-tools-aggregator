# World Model Companies Are Keeping a Lot of Secrets

TechCrunch reported on September 18, 2026 that companies developing world models—AI systems capable of simulating physical environments and real-world processes—are being exceptionally secretive about their actual capabilities and technical approaches.

---

## The Transparency Problem in World Models

World models represent the next frontier in AI development. Unlike language models that process text, world models aim to understand and simulate the underlying physics, causality, and dynamics of the real world. Companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and various startups are investing heavily in this space, but exact technical details remain closely guarded.

According to the TechCrunch report, founders, investors, and even data suppliers are unwilling to disclose what these systems are actually capable of. The secrecy extends beyond typical competitive intelligence protection—some companies have refused to share basic information about training data, model architecture, or evaluation benchmarks.

---

## Why the Secrecy?

Several factors drive this lack of transparency:

**Competitive Advantage**: World models have significant applications in robotics, autonomous vehicles, scientific simulation, and defense. Companies want to maintain lead times over competitors.

**Safety Concerns**: Some in the AI safety community argue that releasing detailed information about world model capabilities could enable misuse. Simulating biological systems or infrastructure could theoretically aid bad actors.

**Investor Pressure**: VC firms funding these projects often prefer secrecy to protect potential IPO timelines or acquisition prospects.

**Regulatory Uncertainty**: With AI regulation evolving rapidly, companies may be waiting to see what disclosure requirements emerge before publishing detailed technical papers.

---

## Industry Implications

The lack of transparency makes it difficult for external researchers, policymakers, and even potential enterprise customers to properly evaluate these systems. Independent benchmarks and red-teaming efforts require access to model details that companies are unwilling to share.

This opacity contrasts with the more open approaches of some academic labs and open-source initiatives. It raises questions about how the AI industry will balance competitive secrecy with the accountability demands of regulators and the public.
