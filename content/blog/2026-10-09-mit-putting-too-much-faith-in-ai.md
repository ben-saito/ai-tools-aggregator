# We're Putting Too Much Faith in AI's Ability to Say No

---

In the summer of 2023, a lawyer filed a brief citing six court cases. None of them existed. The AI system he used had hallucinated the citations, and he hadn't bothered to check. The judge was not amused. This incident became a cautionary tale that the legal profession has been debating ever since.

But the real question isn't why one lawyer made this mistake. It's why we expect individual lawyers—or individual doctors, individual journalists, individual software developers—to do what AI systems demonstrably cannot: reliably distinguish their own correct outputs from their incorrect ones.

The problem runs deeper than carelessness or over-reliance on new tools. Psychologists have documented a persistent human tendency to attribute too much competence to systems that exhibit fluency. We see articulate speech and assume articulate thinking. We see confident assertion and assume reliable knowledge. These heuristics served humans well in dealing with other humans, where confidence does correlate somewhat with competence. They fail catastrophically when applied to systems that can generate fluent nonsense with the same surface characteristics as genuine knowledge.

Research published this year in cognitive science journals documents what many practitioners have suspected: humans show similar trust patterns regardless of whether they're interacting with a human expert or an AI system, at least in brief interactions. The conversational format creates an illusion of understanding that persists even when users are explicitly told the system is unreliable.

What's particularly concerning is that expert users don't perform much better. Studies with physicians, attorneys, and software engineers—all professionals who should know better—show only modest improvements in detecting AI errors compared to laypeople. Domain expertise helps somewhat, but not as much as you'd expect. The AI's fluency consistently creates a baseline of trust that's hard to override with critical thinking.

The implications are uncomfortable. If humans can't reliably detect AI errors even in their own domains of expertise, then deploying these systems in high-stakes domains requires either much better AI—or much more sophisticated human oversight than current practice provides.

Several research groups are working on technical solutions: AI systems that explicitly flag confidence levels, interfaces that make uncertainty visible, and training methods that reward appropriate refusal. But these approaches bump against a fundamental tension: users often prefer confident wrong answers to uncertain ones. The demand for authoritative-sounding outputs drives development toward more confident, not more appropriately uncertain, systems.
