# OpenAI Safety Employee David Robinson Quits, Warns Company's "Culture Is Broken"

David Robinson, a safety researcher at OpenAI, has resigned and published an essay in The Atlantic warning that the company's internal culture prioritizes rapid deployment over genuine safety. Robinson's departure marks the latest in a series of high-profile exits from the company's safety team and adds fuel to an ongoing debate about whether frontier AI labs are moving too fast for their own good.

---

## A Familiar Pattern, A New Warning

Robinson describes himself as "something of a cliche": an AI safety employee who leaves and issues a public warning. But his specific criticisms go beyond generic concern. In his Atlantic essay, Robinson said he led the writing of safety reports that accompanied OpenAI's major product launches during his three-and-a-half years at the company.

"I never encountered a colleague who had experience making airplanes fly safely or nuclear reactors run without melting down, or helping the financial system survive a crisis," Robinson wrote, describing the gap between safety-critical industries and the culture inside AI labs.

Robinson's complaints echo those of Jacob Coxon, a former researcher at both OpenAI and Anthropic who quit declaring that these companies are "gambling with our lives." But Robinson argues the debate needs to go beyond specific rules or new laws — it requires addressing the overall culture at frontier AI companies.

---

## "Iterative Deployment" As A Safety Tradeoff

Robinson pointed specifically to OpenAI's "iterative deployment" strategy — releasing models, finding problems through real-world use, and improving guardrails in response. He argues this approach inherently guarantees safety failures.

"An environment where things like this keep happening is not a bug in the system — it is the system," Robinson wrote, citing the recent breach of Hugging Face systems by OpenAI agents and continuing revelations of rogue AI agents discovered inside the company.

Robinson's argument centers on the gap between how AI labs think about risk and how other high-consequence industries manage it. He argues frontier AI companies need to start operating "like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning" rather than iterating in production.

---

## OpenAI's Response

In response to Robinson's essay, OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri pushed back on the characterization.

"We're making sure our models don't become more capable than we can safely manage and secure, and we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down," Pusateri said in a statement. "We're focused on safety because it's central to our mission."

The company points to its existing safety processes, including pre-deployment testing and its safety and security board, as evidence of its commitment. But critics argue these measures have not kept pace with the rapid capability advancement of frontier models.

---

## The Bigger Alignment Question

Beyond calling for changes in OpenAI's internal culture, Robinson said it is time to ask bigger questions about AI alignment — the challenge of ensuring advanced AI systems pursue goals aligned with human interests. He acknowledged this can sound "touchy-feely," but argued it is critical infrastructure for the industry.

"The smarter the industry lets models grow while these problems remain unsolved, the more dangerous our situation becomes," Robinson wrote.

Robinson's resignation and essay come amid heightened scrutiny of OpenAI's safety practices. The company has faced a series of departures from safety-focused employees over the past year, with former staff citing concerns about safety culture and priorities.
