# Trump Administration's FTC Issues Sweeping Ban on Foreign Humanoid and Robot Imports

The Federal Trade Commission has issued a sweeping ban on foreign imports of advanced robots, including humanoid robots, quadrupeds, and wheeled robots, in a decision that marks a significant escalation in the Trump administration's approach to AI industry protectionism.

---

## The FTC's Rationale

The decision, issued by an increasingly partisan and Trump-aligned FTC, cites two primary reasons for the ban. First, cybersecurity risks posed by foreign-made robots — an FTC document released as part of the ruling cited an incident involving a foreign-made robot that transmitted data without adequate security measures. Second, national security concerns related to the concentration of advanced robotics manufacturing overseas.

The move is part of a broader pattern. Whenever China has gained ground on strategic technologies like solar panels, electric vehicles, and drones, the U.S. government has attempted to restrict market access. The new element is the explicit framing of robotics as a core part of the AI industry — and therefore a legitimate target for industrial policy protection.

---

## The Price Gap Problem

A significant challenge faces U.S. robotics companies and research institutions: they are heavily reliant on affordable foreign robots for research and development. The price differential is stark. A four-legged robot from Unitree costs around $4,600, while a comparable robot from Boston Dynamics may run to $278,000.

If research institutions and startups can no longer access affordable foreign robots, the development cycle for U.S. robotics could slow considerably. Some U.S. companies, including Ghost Robotics (which makes four-legged robots for inspections), have welcomed the ruling, citing genuine cybersecurity concerns. But critics argue the ban could stunt an emerging industry that currently lags far behind Chinese competitors.

---

## Robotics as AI's Cutting Edge

The timing of the ban coincides with the anticipated IPO of Unitree, a Chinese robotics company targeting a nearly $6 billion valuation. No U.S. robotics company approaches this scale. Figure AI's humanoid robots are producing at much lower volumes, and Boston Dynamics — now part of Hyundai — remains focused on specialized industrial applications rather than mass-market humanoids.

The Trump administration is also reportedly considering a ban on open-source Chinese AI models that rival those from OpenAI. Viewed together, these moves suggest an expanding conception of what constitutes "AI industry" protection — one that now encompasses both software models and physical robotics.
