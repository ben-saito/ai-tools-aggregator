# Runlayer and Rippling Drop Lawsuits — But the Brouhaha Is Still a Cautionary Tale for Founders

Runlayer and Rippling have dropped their lawsuits against each other, with no money exchanged. But the incident offers a cautionary example of how quickly founder disputes can become public — and how difficult it is to walk back legal escalation once it happens.

The details of the underlying dispute are not fully public. What is known: Rippling celebrated the resolution by releasing a competing product, a move that observers interpreted as a signal that the lawsuit may have been motivated in part by competitive concerns rather than pure legal merit.

Startups in the HR and IT management space — Rippling's core market — operate in a crowded, fast-moving segment where feature differentiation is difficult and customer lock-in is limited. In that environment, the urge to use legal tools to slow a competitor is understandable, if regrettable.

The episode has reignited debate about the use of litigation as a competitive strategy in the startup world. Some argue that founders have a fiduciary duty to protect their companies by any legal means available; others argue that launching a lawsuit against a well-funded competitor is often a sign that a company is struggling to compete on product merit alone.

In Runlayer's case, the decision to litigate — and then to drop the suit — suggests the company may have miscalculated the reputational and financial cost of prolonged legal combat. The competing product Rippling released in the aftermath may prove more damaging than any temporary injunction would have been.
