# Massachusetts Cracks Down on Data Centers With New Clean Power Rules

Massachusetts has become the third state in as many months to impose new restrictions on data center development, with Governor Maura Healey signing an executive order that requires facilities larger than 25 megawatts of peak demand to supply their own clean power and guarantee it meets the state's clean energy standards.

---

## The Order: Bring Your Own Power

Under Healey's order, data centers above the 25-megawatt threshold must either generate clean power on-site, fund construction of new clean generation nearby, or pay into a ratepayer protection fund. The governor's preference is clearly for on-site generation: the order explicitly states she would prefer facilities to generate clean power onsite.

The order also directs Massachusetts communities to avoid signing non-disclosure agreements with data center developers — a provision designed to increase public transparency around data center deals. The state is also pausing applications for a data center sales tax exemption that only went into effect last month, giving regulators time to implement the new requirements.

---

## 100% Clean Energy Requirement

Massachusetts' clean energy standard already requires industries to source a growing portion of their electricity from approved sources — wind, solar, and hydro — with the minimum increasing each year toward a 40% requirement in 2030. But Healey's office has clarified that data centers will be held to a higher bar: 100% of their electricity demand must be matched with clean energy generation.

This is a materially stricter requirement than the general state standard and represents a significant shift in how Massachusetts treats the energy footprint of digital infrastructure.

---

## A National Pattern: States Pushing Back on Data Centers

Massachusetts is the latest in a rapidly growing list of states pushing back against unrestricted data center development. In August, Texas Governor Greg Abbott announced that all new data centers in the state would need to submit to audits by the public utility commission and ERCOT, the state's grid operator. In July, New York's governor halted construction of new data centers of 50 megawatts or larger.

The pattern reflects a broader reckoning with the energy appetite of AI-driven compute infrastructure. As hyperscalers build out GPU clusters to train and serve large language models, communities and regulators are increasingly questioning the environmental and grid-impact costs.

---

## For Developers: What This Means for AI Infrastructure Planning

For engineering teams and companies building AI applications, the Massachusetts rules signal that cloud region selection may need to account for more than cost and latency. States with aggressive clean energy standards — or those moving in that direction — may become preferred locations for new GPU clusters and training facilities.

The tax exemption pause is also relevant: data center operators had been planning around the recently enacted sales tax exemption, and its sudden suspension creates planning uncertainty. Companies with multi-year infrastructure roadmaps should monitor regulatory developments in the states where they operate or plan to operate.

The broader implication is that AI compute growth is increasingly intersecting with energy policy in ways that could constrain where and how fast new capacity can be built.
