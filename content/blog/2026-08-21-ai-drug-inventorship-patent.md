# When AI Designs a Drug, Who Gets the Credit? The Patent Problem

When biotech company Insilico Medicine used its AI models to propose a promising drug candidate for pulmonary fibrosis, its press release enthusiastically credited the "discovery" to its generative AI platform. But when it came time to file for a patent, the application named only human inventors—five individuals, including CEO Alex Zhavoronkov. No AI appeared on the patent.

---

## The Inventorship Gap

This discrepancy highlights a fundamental tension in intellectual property law. US courts have consistently held that inventors must be human. The 2022 ruling in *Thaler v. Vidal* affirmed that "individual" in patent statutes means a natural person—an AI cannot be named as an inventor regardless of its role in the creative process.

The case originated from a pro bono test brought by attorney Ryan Abbott, who argued that DABUS, an AI system, should be recognized as inventor of a food container design. The DC Circuit Court of Appeals rejected this, citing statutory language and declining to adjudicate what it called "metaphysical matters" about AI consciousness.

---

## Tool vs. Inventor

The Patent and Trademark Office has acknowledged that AI systems "may perform acts that, if performed by a human, could constitute inventorship." The critical distinction is that AI acts as a tool—similar to a laboratory instrument—rather than a legal person capable of holding rights.

This creates practical challenges for AI-driven drug development. Companies like Insilico freely credit their AI platforms in marketing while relying on humaninventor designations for legal protection. This inconsistency could become a vulnerability: patents with incorrectly listed inventors risk invalidation.

---

## Implications for AI-Driven R&D

Abbott warns that excluding AI-generated outputs from patent protection could dampen investment in AI-driven drug development. The US Copyright Office has already moved to refuse copyright protection for AI-generated images and text, raising concerns from film studios and content creators.

The core policy question shifts from whether AI can invent to whether any human contributed sufficiently to warrant inventorship credit—a standard that may prove difficult to apply as AI systems become more autonomous.
