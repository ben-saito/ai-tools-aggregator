# Stanford Study: AI Hits Entry-Level Jobs Hardest

---

## The Employment Shift Nobody Predicted

When researchers first warned about AI's impact on jobs, the conventional wisdom focused on automating routine physical and cognitive tasks — work typically done by experienced professionals. But a new Stanford study published in August 2026 finds the reality is inverted: AI is hitting entry-level positions hardest, while jobs requiring more experience have proven more resistant to displacement.

The research, titled "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence," analyzed anonymized payroll data from ADP covering millions of workers. The team rated occupations based on their exposure to AI automation, then tracked employment trends across age groups and experience levels.

---

## The Numbers: Entry-Level Down 19%

For workers aged 22-25 in AI-impacted fields, the findings are stark. Entry-level employment in occupations classified as highly exposed to AI has declined 19% compared to positions in fields more resistant to AI disruption. The researchers, led by Erik Brynjolfsson, used Anthropic's Economic Index to classify which tasks involve "automative" AI — technology that replaces human labor rather than augmenting it.

The pattern holds across the board: younger workers in their first jobs are bearing a disproportionate share of AI-driven displacement. The study found that occupations with high concentrations of automative AI use are the ones showing the worst relative employment levels for newcomers.

---

## Why Entry-Level Work Is Most Vulnerable

The explanation lies in the nature of early career work. Entry-level positions typically involve codified knowledge: structured tasks with clear procedures, documented processes, and learnable rules. This is exactly the kind of work that large language models do best.

"Tasks that are automative in nature are the ones displacing young workers," the researchers note. The study used formal education requirements in the O*NET occupational database as a proxy for how much a job relies on tacit, experience-based knowledge versus learnable procedures. The more codified the work, the more vulnerable it is to AI replacement.

More experienced workers, by contrast, typically spend a larger portion of their time on judgment calls, relationship management, and context-dependent decisions — skills that require human intuition and remain harder to automate.

---

## The Broader Economic Picture

Interestingly, when looking at the economy as a whole, the researchers found relatively muted effects. Overall employment differences between highly AI-impacted and AI-resistant fields are small. The researchers attribute this to the countervailing forces of job displacement and new job creation partially offsetting each other.

But the distributional effect — the way those impacts concentrate among younger workers entering the labor market — is what makes the study's findings so significant. The entry-level job market isn't just smaller; it's shrinking in precisely the segment where workers would normally build the experience needed for advancement.

---

## Implications for Policy and Education

The study raises uncomfortable questions about who bears the cost of AI adoption. If entry-level positions serve as the primary training ground for career development, their displacement could have long-term consequences for labor market mobility and wage progression.

The researchers suggest that education and training systems may need to adapt, focusing less on codified procedural knowledge and more on the judgment and relational skills that keep experienced workers employed. Whether policy can meaningfully address the structural shift underway remains an open question.
