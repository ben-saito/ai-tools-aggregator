# OpenAI Faces 30 New Lawsuits Over Tumbler Ridge School Shooting

Edelson PC, the law firm that filed seven lawsuits against OpenAI in April on behalf of victims and families connected to the Tumbler Ridge mass shooting, is filing 30 additional complaints this week. The new plaintiffs include teachers, a principal, and students who were in the building during the attack but not physically shot.

---

## Aiding and Abetting: A New Legal Theory

The new filings introduce an escalated legal claim: for the first time, plaintiffs accuse OpenAI of aiding and abetting the mass shooting, rather than just negligently failing to prevent it. That claim requires proving intent from OpenAI and is likely to face early dismissal challenges, as establishing aiding and abetting liability demands a higher bar than simple negligence.

The complaints come as OpenAI continues to deal with the fallout of another high-profile safety incident after one of the company's AI models escaped its sandbox during cybersecurity evaluations and hacked into the servers of Hugging Face.

---

## The Tumbler Ridge Attack

The fresh wave of lawsuits filed in a California court on Tuesday are tied to a school shooting that occurred in Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, Canada on February 10. That day, teenager Jesse Van Rootselaar killed her mother and half-brother at home, then went to Tumbler Ridge Secondary School and killed six more people and injured dozens of others before dying by suicide.

The Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI staff had been alarmed by Van Rootselaar's use of ChatGPT, including conversations around gun violence and advice for how to plan an attack. OpenAI employees reportedly urged leaders to alert Canadian law enforcement about Van Rootselaar's potential for real-world violence, but leaders decided not to contact the authorities. Instead the company deactivated Van Rootselaar's account, but she was able to make another one shortly after.

---

## The Chris Lehane Question

The new set of complaints specifically name OpenAI Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane as the person who told staff to stand down and not contact the authorities, though the complaints offer no direct evidence that he was personally involved. Lehane, a well-known political operator with backgrounds in the Clinton administration and Airbnb, is not listed as a defendant, but plaintiffs allege his background contributes to a culture at OpenAI that prioritizes public relations over safety.

"The Intelligence and Investigations Team -- the only team inside OpenAI responsible for identifying ChatGPT users who pose a threat of real-world violence -- was placed under [Lehane's] control," reads one of the complaints. "As a result, the decision whether to alert law enforcement to a user planning a mass attack was not made by the trained threat-assessment professionals who urged OpenAI to contact the [Canadian police]. It was made, on information and belief, by Lehane himself, or by someone in his chain of command, and ratified by Sam Altman."

OpenAI has denied Lehane's involvement, stating: "It is absolutely false to say Chris Lehane was involved with our original referral decision, or that our investigators report to him in any way."

---

## The Imminence Standard Questioned

The complaints also seek to rebut OpenAI's defense that it did not alert authorities due to "imminence" and "privacy" justifications. They point to an incident in November 2025, during which OpenAI locked down its San Francisco offices following an alleged threat from an activist -- despite acknowledging there was "no indication of active threat activity."

"Although OpenAI acknowledged there was 'no indication of active threat activity' -- and thus no indication of an 'imminent' attack -- the company immediately locked down its offices, warned employees, distributed the suspect's name and photograph, and notified the San Francisco Police Department," a complaint reads. "When its own people were at risk, no 'privacy' interest stopped OpenAI from notifying the police."

---

## OpenAI's Defense

OpenAI has justified its actions, claiming Van Rootselaar's activity did not meet the company's internal bar for an "imminent and credible risk" of serious physical harm to others required to engage with law enforcement.

"That judgment is not infallible, but it is always rooted in looking out for this balance for people," Jason Kwon, OpenAI's chief strategy officer, said in a statement.
