# OpenAI Navier-Stokes controversy: NYU mathematician claims lab accessed his unpublished math work

When a top-tier AI lab races to solve one of mathematics' most celebrated unsolved problems, the speed of its breakthrough can raise as many questions as the breakthrough itself. That tension is now playing out publicly between OpenAI and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, with buckmaster accusing the lab of building on his unpublished research without proper acknowledgment.

---

## The Navier-Stokes problem and its $1 million prize

The Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems — a set of the most consequential unsolved questions in mathematics, each carrying a $1 million bounty from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The equations describe fluid dynamics and are foundational to physics, yet their mathematical properties remain poorly understood in theoretical terms. A complete proof would represent a major advance in mathematical physics.

Buckmaster, working with Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge, had been pursuing a solution to the problem over an extended period. Their work was approaching publication when OpenAI announced its own independent proof of the central conjecture.

---

## The accusation: premature access and unfair advantage

According to Buckmaster's public statement, he and his collaborators learned that "information about our progress had been passed to OpenAI" before their work became public. When they contacted OpenAI, they asked how the lab had begun its research and how much human input was involved in its proof. The answers they received, Buckmaster says, were unsatisfactory.

Shortly after Buckmaster's announcement, OpenAI published a full proof claiming to have solved Navier-Stokes, attributing the work to an unreleased next-generation model. The company said the model had tackled a range of unsolved problems over the course of a week, consuming 300 billion output tokens — roughly $22.5 million worth of compute at current Astra pricing.

The core dispute centers on whether OpenAI's proof work began after receiving unauthorized access to Buckmaster's pre-publication mathematical progress, and whether the lab's response to questions about the timeline was forthcoming.

---

## OpenAI's response and the broader context

OpenAI has acknowledged the controversy but pointed to its general research process for unsolved problems. The incident echoes a broader pattern in the AI industry: frontier labs pursuing mathematical and scientific breakthroughs at extraordinary compute cost, sometimes with limited transparency about their methods or data sources.

For the AI research community, the episode highlights the growing tension between proprietary model development and academic norms around pre-publication sharing. Mathematical research traditionally relies on a culture of open progress updates and peer review — norms that can conflict with the fast-moving, secretive pace of frontier AI development.

---

## What this means for AI and mathematics

Whatever the outcome of this particular dispute, the episode underscores that AI systems are increasingly capable of contributing to frontier mathematical research. Whether that contribution comes through genuine autonomous reasoning or through pattern-matching on vast corpora of existing mathematical literature remains an open question — and one that becomes more pressing as labs compete to claim breakthroughs at the highest levels of mathematics.

The Clay Mathematics Institute has not yet formally evaluated either proof. The mathematical community will ultimately determine which contribution, if either, constitutes a valid solution to the Navier-Stokes problem.
