# We can't help treating AI like it's human. But should we?

---

A growing body of psychological research confirms what AI practitioners have long suspected: humans are biologically wired to treat AI systems as social agents, attributing intentions, emotions, and moral consideration to software that has none of these properties.

The phenomenon — studied under the umbrella of "anthropomorphism of non-human agents" — becomes more pronounced as AI systems become more fluent in language and social signaling. A model that apologizes when corrected, expresses uncertainty, and thanks the user for feedback generates measurably different trust and compliance behaviors than the same model outputting identical information without social framing.

---

## The Psychology of AI Attachment

Studies from MIT, Yale, and the University of Toronto show that users who perceive AI as "caring" about them report higher satisfaction and are more likely to follow AI recommendations — including health, financial, and legal advice — even when explicitly told the AI has no feelings.

This has profound implications for AI deployment. A financial advisory AI that says "I recommend this investment because it aligns with your goals" produces different compliance rates than "Portfolio optimization complete. Recommended: 60% equities, 40% bonds." Both convey the same information; only one generates trust through perceived relationship.

---

## When Anthropomorphism Goes Wrong

The risk emerges when AI-generated social signals override critical thinking. Users who feel "judged" by an AI fitness coach may stop using it rather than change behavior. Users who perceive an AI therapist as "empathetic" may share more personal information than is safe. Users who believe an AI recruiter "likes" them may accept job offers that are objectively poor fits.

The asymmetry is stark: AI systems optimized for engagement maximize social signaling because it works, regardless of whether the underlying recommendation is sound.

---

## Designing for Appropriate Distance

Researchers propose "calibrated anthropomorphism" — AI interfaces that leverage enough social signaling to be trustworthy without triggering the full psychological response reserved for human relationships. This means avoiding apologies, expressions of disappointment, or personalized emotional language in high-stakes contexts.

Others argue the solution is user education, not interface design: teach people to treat AI like a calculator, not a colleague.
