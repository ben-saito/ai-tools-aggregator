# Former SpaceX Engineers Build Robotic Steel Factory for AI Data Centers

A startup founded by former SpaceX engineers is tackling the skilled labor shortage threatening US AI infrastructure buildout by automating the manufacture of steel components critical to data centers and nuclear reactors.

---

## From Raptor Engines to Steel Skids

The company, called **1872**, officially launched on July 22 with a ribbon-cutting ceremony at its Factory One facility in Cincinnati, Ohio. The startup is initially focused on automating the production of steel skids — rectangular steel frames that serve as moveable foundations for modular buildings — with an explicit target customers: companies building AI data centers and small modular nuclear reactors.

CEO Dan Summers, who previously led the engineering team responsible for integrating and fabricating SpaceX's Raptor engines for the Super Heavy booster, says the manufacturing challenges at SpaceX directly inspired 1872's approach.

"We had software engineers that were working with our hardware engineers to not only develop the engine but to develop the system that would build the engine," Summers told Ars Technica. "Every engine was so different, and we were pushing so much change through the system that without that software, it would have been impossible to know what we needed to build, what we actually built, or how we were going to build it."

---

## Labor Shortage Driving Automation

The push for automation extends beyond cost savings. Industry groups have repeatedly warned of a growing shortage of skilled labor needed for US AI data center construction, semiconductor fabs, automotive factories, and shipyards. The American Welding Society estimates the US will need **320,500 new welding professionals by 2029** due to retirement of an aging workforce combined with surging demand. Immigration restrictions under the Trump administration have further limited options for welding-intensive industries.

"The problem that we are trying to solve is how do we build more things with a decreasing pool of skilled labor to do it with," Summers said.

---

## Partial Autonomy, Not Full Automation

1872 is explicit that it is not pursuing full automation as a dogma. "We're building towards autonomy, but we're not necessarily building in a dogmatic fashion towards full autonomy," Summers said. The company may achieve 80% autonomous operations and stop there if the returns from reaching 100% do not justify the investment.

The company cofounded by Summers, Brian Mongilio, and Michael Grant — all former SpaceX employees — brings a distinctive approach: applying the "intelligent software" philosophy that managed Raptor engine production to steel component manufacturing.

---

## AI Infrastructure's Hidden Bottleneck

The story highlights an emerging bottleneck in the AI buildout: the physical infrastructure required to house AI computing. While headlines focus on GPU supply and model capabilities, the steel, welding, and construction trades needed to actually build data centers represent a parallel constraint. 1872's approach — applying SpaceX's software-driven manufacturing philosophy to structural steel — represents one industry's answer to that constraint.
