# Anthropic's $2 Trillion IPO Puts External Trustees in the Spotlight

Anthropic's anticipated stock market debut, potentially valuing the AI safety company at $2 trillion, has brought unusual governance mechanisms into sharp focus. The company's Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT) - a small group of external advisers tasked with safeguarding Anthropic's mission of developing AI for humanity's long-term benefit - will play a central role in the company's public market era.

---

## The Long-Term Benefit Trust Structure

The LTBT serves as the controlling governance mechanism of Anthropic's public benefit corporation structure. Unlike typical corporate trustees who focus on shareholder returns, the LTBT is designed to ensure Anthropic remains committed to its founding mission even as commercial pressures intensify.

The trust holds no equity in Anthropic but wields significant influence. It has the right to appoint or dismiss the majority of Anthropic's board, providing a structural check on decisions that might prioritize short-term profits over long-term safety considerations. Currently, the LTBT has three members out of a potential maximum of five.

---

## Leadership of the Trust

The LTBT is chaired by Neil Buddy Shah, chief executive of the Clinton Health Access Initiative, alongside former Federal Reserve chair Ben Bernanke. The composition of the trust reflects Anthropic's approach of blending expertise in global health, economic policy, and technology governance.

This governance structure represents an experiment in balancing commercial imperatives with mission-driven oversight. As Anthropic navigates the expectations and scrutiny of public markets, the trust provides a mechanism to preserve decision-making independence from typical shareholder pressure.

---

## Implications for AI Safety Governance

The structure has drawn attention from AI policy experts and governance scholars. Traditional corporate governance mechanisms typically prioritize shareholder value, but Anthropic's approach attempts to embed safety considerations directly into governance structures that survive the transition to public ownership.

The arrangement is particularly relevant given ongoing debates about AI safety, alignment research, and the appropriate boundaries for frontier AI development. Anthropic has positioned itself as a safety-first AI company, and the LTBT provides institutional backing for that positioning.

As other AI companies consider public offerings, Anthropic's approach may serve as a template for balancing commercial success with mission preservation. The effectiveness of this structure will likely be studied closely by policymakers, investors, and other AI developers wrestling with similar governance challenges.
