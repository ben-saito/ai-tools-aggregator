# Meta Is Quietly Deploying Robots in Its Data Centers

Meta is testing robots from multiple vendors — Watney Robotics, Kinova, and ABB — for tasks like power cycling servers, swapping networking cables, and cabling work. In one experiment, a Kinova Gen3 robotic arm is being evaluated for cutting electricity to servers, while a different robot is being tested to swap networking cables. One Meta data center worker estimates that if the technology is successful, the bot could replace up to 80 percent of some people's workloads. "We thought those of us performing the physical tasks were safe for a while, but not anymore," the worker said. "It is coming for us all, unfortunately."

---

## From Tugger Bots to Dual-Armed Cabling Robots

Meta's automation push in data centers is not entirely new. In a 2023 YouTube video, the company showcased a self-driving tugger robot for transporting heavy server racks and a wheeled barcode-reading robot for inventory tracking. Both are now operating in a number of Meta's data centers, including facilities in Iowa and Virginia, according to people familiar with the projects. The inventory bot has expanded into failure inspections, though its grayscale-only camera cannot differentiate between green and red indicator lights, meaning human checks remain necessary for some breakdowns scenarios. The device also struggles with corners and traversing cables in its path.

Since last June, Meta has been trying out a pair of dual-armed Watney robots for cabling work at its Altoona, Iowa campus. The robots are supervised by humans and cannot yet work as fast as people, but they show promise, according to one person familiar with the company's efforts. Meta is also testing ABB four-wheel robots with a scissor-lift-style riser and a six-axis arm at its Prometheus campus in New Albany, Ohio. These robots are being used to reseat parts and may take on other tasks with diminishing human oversight over time.

---

## The Hardware Has Finally Caught Up

Until recently, robots were not well suited for the delicate work happening in data centers. They were expensive, and in earlier industry trials they sometimes crushed servers while attempting simple tasks, a person briefed on the experimentation said. But hardware costs have declined, and AI models powering robots are now far more capable than they were a few years ago. Several startups are working to build commercial robots capable of assembling servers, swapping parts, and replacing wires.

At a conference last year, Eric Xu, senior manager for robotics at Meta, outlined the company's long-term goals: deploying robots in data centers to speed up incident response, monitor the environment, and handle preventative maintenance. "We believe more collaboration and research will be needed, but we have to start, otherwise we do not have a chance to do this," he said.

---

## "Smart Hands" vs. Skilled Workers

Not all Meta data center workers see the robotics push as positive. Two workers described a trend where Meta is developing AI software that they believe will allow the company to hire lower-skilled, lower-paid workers and centralize some roles in areas like Denver, Colorado. "They no longer want people who can think independently, come up with creative solutions, or perform complex troubleshooting," one worker said. "They want 'smart hands,' people who are just capable enough to follow AI instructions and not mess anything up."

Meta contends its need for skilled workers remains robust. This year, the company launched a program to train thousands of people annually in electrical, mechanical, and plumbing work at no cost, with job guarantees in states including Louisiana, Ohio, Indiana, and Texas. The company has also partnered with building trade unions to fund apprenticeship programs.

---

## Industry-Wide Trend: Robots for Data Center Operations

Meta is not alone. Microsoft and Google have separately invested in robots for their data center operations, and Amazon has discussed using robots to help recycle parts at its facilities. Paul Golding, who oversees physical intelligence at Analog Devices, said more customers are requesting humanoid robots for data centers because they can run at higher temperatures, in the dark, and in environments inhospitable to human workers.

The prospect of robots replacing at least some data center jobs may need to become a bigger concern for communities that grant tax breaks to tech companies. Altoona Mayor Dean O'Connor said he has not given much thought to robots taking jobs, though he frames automation as inevitable. "We will deal with it as it comes," he said.
