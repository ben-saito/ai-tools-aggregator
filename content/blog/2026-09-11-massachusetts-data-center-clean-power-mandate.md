# Massachusetts Data Centers Face New Clean Power Mandate

Massachusetts has become the third state in as many months to impose new restrictions on data center development, but with a significant twist that sets it apart from earlier measures. Governor Maura Healey's executive order requires developers building data centers larger than 25 megawatts of peak demand to provide their own power and guarantee it adheres to the state's clean energy requirements.

---

## A New Approach to Data Center Regulation

Under Healey's order, large data center operators must either generate clean power on-site, fund construction of new renewable generation nearby, or pay into a ratepayer protection fund. The mandate marks a departure from the incentive-heavy approach that once characterized state efforts to attract data center investment.

"Just a few years ago, tech companies and data center developers were showered with incentives to locate facilities in a particular state," one industry observer noted. "Now they're fighting a groundswell of public opposition as politicians seek to show voters they're addressing their concerns."

The 25-megawatt threshold is designed to target hyperscale facilities rather than smaller operations, focusing on the large-scale AI infrastructure driving most of the new demand.

---

## States Are Shifting Their Approach

Massachusetts joins Virginia and Georgia as states that have moved to restrict data center development in recent months. Virginia, home to a significant concentration of data center infrastructure in Loudoun County, has faced growing pushback from local communities concerned about energy consumption and environmental impact.

The Massachusetts order also includes a provision directing communities to "avoid signing non-disclosure agreements," a move aimed at increasing transparency around data center development deals that have often been conducted in private.

---

## AI Infrastructure Boom Drives New Policy Responses

The surge in AI-related computing demand has accelerated data center construction across the United States. Tech companies building large language models and AI systems require substantial power capacity, leading to increased scrutiny of the environmental and grid impacts of new facilities.

The policy shift reflects growing tension between the economic benefits of data center investment and community concerns about energy costs, land use, and environmental sustainability. As AI infrastructure needs continue to grow, more states are expected to examine regulatory approaches that balance these competing interests.
