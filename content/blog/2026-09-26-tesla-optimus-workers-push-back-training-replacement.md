# Tesla Workers Push Back on Training Optimus Robots for Replacement

Tesla workers at the company's Fremont factory have resisted assignments to train the company's Optimus humanoid robots, with some telling The Information that they knew the robots were being developed to eventually replace their jobs. The report comes as Tesla faces mounting challenges bringing Optimus from prototype to mass production.

---

## Worker Resistance and Data Collection Strategy Shift

Tesla has been using imitation learning to train Optimus, requiring human workers to perform tasks while wearing motion-capture suits so the robots can learn through observation. Workers at the Fremont factory in California and other facilities have been involved in this data collection process. But according to The Information, some workers complained that they were essentially training their own replacements.

Tesla has since shifted to dedicated training teams and established specialized training hubs, removing the responsibility from regular production workers. This change appears designed to reduce friction and improve the quality of training data by using workers specifically hired for the task rather than repurposed automotive line workers.

The broader challenge is that Optimus requires massive amounts of training data for manipulation tasks that humans find trivial. Getting robots to reliably grasp and handle objects with the dexterity of human hands has proven one of the most difficult engineering problems in robotics.

---

## Production Challenges and Manufacturing Hurdles

Tesla's Optimus V3 has faced significant development and manufacturing difficulties. The company has struggled with production line equipment that cannot precisely align components, a critical requirement for humanoid robot assembly. Unlike specialized industrial robots that perform one repetitive task, Optimus is designed to handle a wide variety of general-purpose operations.

Manufacturing robot hands capable of delicate manipulation has been particularly problematic. Tesla's solution has been to develop a glove-like layer of touch sensors that can be replaced without disassembling the entire hand. The company has also had to manually assemble some components that automation cannot yet handle reliably.

The result is that newly produced Optimus robots require immediate fixes after coming off the line, adding to manufacturing costs and timelines. Tesla aims to produce 1,000 Optimus units per week by the end of 2026, a target that outside observers view with skepticism given current production realities.

---

## Supply Chain Dependence on China

Tesla's Optimus program relies heavily on Chinese suppliers for robot components, a dependency that has persisted despite the Trump administration's efforts to boost domestic supply chains. In July 2026, the Federal Communications Commission banned new foreign-made humanoid robots and four-legged robot dogs, a policy move targeting Chinese robotics imports.

This creates a dilemma for Tesla: domestic alternatives are limited, and the company's cost structure depends on Chinese manufacturing for many specialized components. The FCC ban complicates future procurement even as it aims to protect US robotics companies from foreign competition.

---

## Competitive Landscape

Tesla is not alone in pursuing general-purpose humanoid robots. Agility Robotics, based in Oregon, is among the companies furthest along in commercial deployment, with robots already working in a Georgia warehouse owned by GXO Logistics. Chinese, Japanese, and South Korean automakers and robotics companies are also developing humanoid robots, creating a competitive environment where Tesla must prove its robots can outperform alternatives.

The fundamental question remains whether humanoid robots can achieve the reliability and cost-effectiveness needed for widespread commercial adoption. Tesla's approach of pushing rapidly toward mass production contrasts with more conservative development timelines at competitors.
