# Large Genome Models Used to Design Genetically Distant Viruses

AI models trained on DNA sequences can now design complete viral genomes. Researchers at Stanford University have demonstrated that large genome models Evo 1 and Evo 2 can output the genetic sequences of bacteriophages—viruses that infect bacteria—producing genetically distant versions of existing viruses that retain infectivity while acquiring novel features.

---

## From Language to Genomes

Large genome models apply the same fundamental approach as language models: predict the next token in a sequence. For DNA, this means predicting the next base—A, T, C, or G—in a genome sequence. Unlike natural language, where context determines word choice, genomes have regions where the next base is functionally critical interspersed with regions where any base would work equally well. Despite this complexity, training on enough genome sequences enables these models to recognize biological context even in areas humans haven't fully characterized.

The researchers first discovered that large genome models could output DNA sequences encoding functional proteins. Prompted with partial gene clusters, the models produce sequences encoding proteins with related functions—potentially including novel proteins that resemble nothing previously identified. This capability raised an immediate question: could these models output entire viral genomes?

---

## Designing ΦX174

The team selected bacteriophage ΦX174 as their test case. This virus, which infects E. coli, has well-characterized biology: 11 genes spread over approximately 5,400 bases, all gene functions identified, and a fully mapped infection cycle. Using Evo 1 and Evo 2, the researchers prompted the models with sequences from the ΦX174 family and asked them to generate genetically distant relatives—viruses related enough to be recognizable but distant enough to test the model's generalization capability.

The results showed that large genome models can successfully design functional viral genomes that differ meaningfully from their training distribution. The generated viruses maintain infectivity while exhibiting features that would be challenging to evolve through natural selection alone. This demonstrates that genome models aren't merely memorizing training sequences—they're learning underlying biological principles that allow genuine creative design.

---

## Safety Considerations and Viral-Host Range

The researchers deliberately excluded viral sequences targeting complex eukaryotic cells (including humans) from model training. While this restriction doesn't eliminate risk—it limits rather than removes the possibility of future models being trained on broader viral data—it reflects responsible consideration of dual-use potential. The work highlights how AI-assisted biosecurity threats require governance frameworks that account for rapidly evolving capabilities.

The immediate scope remains constrained to bacteriophages, which cannot infect human cells. However, the fundamental capability being demonstrated—AI systems designing genetic sequences with biological function—points toward a future where similar approaches might be applied to viruses with different host ranges. The researchers explicitly note that their work should prompt forward-looking discussions about AI biosecurity governance.

---

## Implications for AI in Biology

This work represents a concrete demonstration of AI moving beyond pattern recognition toward genuine design capability in biology. Large genome models can now function as creative partners in genetic engineering, proposing novel sequences that achieve specified biological functions. For drug discovery, agricultural biotechnology, and synthetic biology, this represents a significant acceleration in the design-build-test cycle for genetically engineered organisms.

The research also surfaces questions about evaluation and oversight. Traditional biosafety protocols weren't designed for an era where viral sequences can be generated by an API call. As these capabilities mature, the research community and regulators will need new frameworks for assessing and managing the risks of AI-designed biological sequences.
