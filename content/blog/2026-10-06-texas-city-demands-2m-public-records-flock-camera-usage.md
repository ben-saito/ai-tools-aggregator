# Texas City Demands $2M for Public Records on Flock Camera Usage

A Texas city has demanded $2 million in public records fees to fulfill a request for documents detailing how police use Flock Safety cameras in the city — a figure that highlights the growing tension between surveillance technology vendors and public accountability laws.

---

## Public Records Dispute

The records request, submitted by a civil liberties organization, sought contracts, usage logs, and internal communications about the city's deployment of Flock's automated license plate reader (ALPR) cameras. The city's response quoted $2 million in fees, citing the volume of data involved and the staff time required to review materials for applicable exemptions.

Flock Safety cameras have been deployed in hundreds of cities across the United States, automatically capturing and cataloging license plates and vehicle characteristics. The data is stored in Flock's cloud infrastructure and made accessible to law enforcement without warrants in many jurisdictions.

---

## Growing Backlash Against Surveillance Costs

The Texas case is part of a broader backlash against the costs — both financial and civil liberties — of automated surveillance systems. Beyond the direct fees, critics point to the long-term consequences of building out surveillance infrastructure that accumulates data on innocent people's movements.

Flock has faced legal challenges in several states over whether its data retention practices comply with public records laws and constitutional limits on warrantless searches.

---

## Model for Obstruction

Good Government groups say the $2 million fee quote is designed to discourage the request rather than reflect genuine cost recovery. The strategy — pricing transparency out of reach for advocacy organizations and journalists — has been used by other vendors facing public records requests about surveillance technology.

The city has offered to waive fees for a reduced request, but advocates say the full scope of the deployment cannot be understood without the complete documentation.

---

*The $2 million fee demand for Flock records reflects a pattern where surveillance technology vendors and municipalities use cost recovery rules to limit public oversight of automated monitoring systems.*
