# Space Mirrors Could Brighten the Night Sky for Millions — And Astronomers Are Worried

A company planning to deploy space mirrors — reflective structures in orbit designed to bounce sunlight to Earth — has disclosed plans that have astronomers alarmed: the mirrors could unintentionally brighten the night sky by a magnitude visible to the naked eye.

The concept, sometimes called solar reflection or orbital solar power, involves positioning large reflective arrays in orbit to direct sunlight to specific ground locations. Proponents argue it could provide renewable energy during nighttime hours or illuminate disaster zones. The company pursuing this technology has not been publicly identified in the Ars Technica report, but the disclosure has raised fresh concerns about the commercialization of low Earth orbit.

Astronomers warn that even a small increase in sky brightness — on the order of a few percent — could interfere with ground-based telescopes and obscure faint celestial objects. Light pollution is already a major concern for observatories; adding a controllable, moveable light source in orbit would compound the problem in unpredictable ways.

The International Astronomical Union has previously raised concerns about satellite mega-constellations like Starlink affecting observations, but space mirrors represent a qualitatively different challenge: where Starlink satellites produce point-source interference, a large mirror could produce diffuse, area-wide brightening.

No regulatory framework currently addresses orbital solar reflectors. The Outer Space Treaty governs some aspects of space activity, but space commercialization — particularly by private companies — has outpaced the development of specific rules. The situation may require new international coordination before the mirrors are deployed.

For now, the astronomical community is watching closely — and hoping the conversation happens before the mirrors go up.
