# US-China Drone and Robot Barriers: Global Market Fragmentation Looms

*The U.S. is building barriers around drones and robots, but China has scale to get around them*

Washington is tightening restrictions on Chinese-made drones and advanced robots in July and August 2026, citing national security concerns. The FCC's Covered List, established in 2021 and initially targeting Huawei, ZTE, and Hikvision, has expanded to foreign-made drones and most recently to advanced robotic devices.

---

## Background: Why Drones and Robots Now

Two actions in July-August 2026: steep tariffs on imported drones and components (effective September, with additional component tariffs in 2027), and the addition of humanoid robots to the FCC Covered List.

Industry analysts describe an already-split drone market: a U.S.-led market built around NDAA-compliant systems, and a China-led market focused on low-cost, high-volume production.

---

## The Data: China's Scale Advantage

According to Counterpoint Research, global humanoid robot shipments hit 22,000 units in the first half of 2026, with Chinese manufacturers accounting for 86%. The five largest makers by shipments — AgiBot, Unitree, Galbot, UBTECH, and Leju Robotics — are all Chinese.

U.S. companies operate at a far smaller scale and struggle to match Chinese pricing, said Soumen Mandal, principal analyst at Counterpoint Research.

Unlike semiconductors, robotics does not depend on a single technology that one country can easily control, said Ankur Saxena, investment director at TDK Ventures.

---

## China's Cost Advantage: Why the US Can't Keep Up

Chinese humanoid makers are bringing more of the technology stack in-house, drawing on China's existing manufacturing base. Unitree is developing more components internally. XPeng and other automakers are transferring experience in chips and vehicle manufacturing to robotics.

This allows Chinese companies to cut humanoid prices faster than U.S. competitors can match.

"You cannot sanction your way around a cost curve. You can only out-build it, and America has yet to begin making the decade-long investment that will require," Saxena said.

Key Chinese players:
- **Unitree**: internalizing component development for cost reduction
- **XPeng**: horizontal expansion from vehicle manufacturing to robotics
- **AgiBot / Galbot / UBTECH / Leju**: together account for 86% of global shipments

---

## The Next Battlefield: Energy and Payload Architecture

"The next competitive frontier is shifting from the drones themselves to who owns the next-gen energy and payload architecture," said Bentzion Levinson, founder and CEO of Heven AeroTech. Battery constraints mean power systems are becoming a key competition point.

Agility Robotics welcomed the FCC decision, pointing to its Digit humanoid (designed and assembled in the U.S.) while calling for continued access to tools and technologies needed for robotics research.

---

## The Future: Fragmented Markets

Industry analysts expect Chinese robotics companies to follow a path similar to Chinese electric-vehicle companies: build scale at home, expand into overseas markets, and eventually establish local production. Price-sensitive markets with severe labor shortages across Europe, Southeast Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East are already targets.

The result may not be two cleanly separated U.S.- and China-led robotics industries. Instead, restrictions could accelerate regional markets: Chinese companies competing on cost and scale, U.S. and allied manufacturers gaining ground where security requirements matter most, and manufacturers in Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea carving out space between the two.

"The alternative to China isn't a purely domestic U.S. supply chain — it's a diversified allied one," Saxena said.

Toyota (Japan) and Hyundai (South Korea, owner of Boston Dynamics) are investing in humanoid robots, but replacing China is not straightforward given how deeply Chinese components remain embedded across the global robotics industry.

---

## Conclusion

U.S. regulations may exclude Chinese manufacturers from the American market, but they do not directly address China's global manufacturing scale and cost advantages. The competition result may be a quieter fragmentation: Chinese companies expanding globally on cost and scale, U.S. and allied manufacturers competing where security requirements matter most.

Saxena's observation — "you cannot sanction your way around a cost curve" — cuts to the fundamental contradiction in the technology race for robotics supremacy.
