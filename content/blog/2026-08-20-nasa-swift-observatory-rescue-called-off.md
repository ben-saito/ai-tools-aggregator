# NASA Calls Off Rescue Mission for Swift Gamma-Ray Observatory

NASA has officially called off efforts to rescue the Swift Gamma-Ray Burst Observatory, ending what would have been the first commercial satellite rescue of a NASA science mission. The announcement on August 19 marks the end of a months-long effort to save the 22-year-old telescope from an inevitable orbital decay and atmospheric reentry.

---

## Background: The Rescue That Never Happened

Swift, launched in November 2004 with a designed mission lifetime of just two years, far exceeded expectations by operating for more than two decades. The spacecraft played a central role in gamma-ray burst science, detecting and characterizing some of the most energetic events in the universe. By mid-2026, however, the observatory had lost attitude control capability due to reaction wheel failures, and its orbit was decaying faster than predicted.

In July 2026, NASA contracted Katalyst Space Technologies to attempt a rescue using the company's Link servicing satellite. The $30 million mission would have marked the first time a commercial entity rescued a NASA science satellite. But Katalyst confirmed on August 19 that the effort had been abandoned due to technical complications with the Link spacecraft's propulsion system.

---

## What Comes Next

Without intervention, Swift is now expected to reenter Earth's atmosphere sometime in late 2026. The spacecraft will largely burn up on reentry, though some debris could reach the surface. Scientific operations have already ceased as the spacecraft can no longer maintain its pointing orientation.

The incident highlights the growing challenge of maintaining aging space infrastructure. Unlike satellites in GEO, low-Earth orbit observatories have no automated reboosting capability, and the commercial satellite servicing industry is still in its infancy.

---

## Implications for AI Data Center Expansion

While Swift's fate is separate from the ongoing AI infrastructure buildout, the observatory's end coincides with an era of unprecedented demand for orbital slots and power allocation. The same broadband satellite mega-constellations being deployed for AI data center connectivity are also complicating the orbital environment for scientific spacecraft.
