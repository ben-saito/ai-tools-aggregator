# DEF CON Attendees Suspected in Fake Wi-Fi Hotspot Attack on Delta Flight

A Delta flight from Las Vegas to Atlanta was the subject of an FBI investigation after passengers allegedly created a fake in-flight Wi-Fi network to harvest credentials. The incident occurred on August 11, 2026, one day after the DEF CON security conference concluded in Las Vegas.

---

## The Incident

The attack was first flagged through ACARS (Aircraft Communications Addressing and Reporting System), a publicly available air-to-ground messaging system. According to the "ACARS Drama" account on social media, pilots reported: "NO INFO AS OF NOW WE HAVE A BUNCH OF PAX THAT WERE AT A CYBER CONFERENCE IN LAS THEY WERE ABLE TO JAM OUR WIFI AND BROADCAST THEIR SIGNAL."

Passengers created a fake hotspot under the name "Delta WiFi Fast," complete with a phishing landing page designed to harvest personal credentials. The technique, known as an "evil twin" attack, is a well-documented method in the IT security community where a malicious actor sets up a counterfeit Wi-Fi network to intercept login data and other sensitive information.

---

## Industry Response

Delta confirmed the incident in a statement: "One initial finding is an unauthorized WiFi network, which was not provided, operated, or supplied by Delta, was present onboard the aircraft for a short time during the flight." The airline emphasized that flight safety was "never in question and no aircraft operating systems were affected," with no emergency declared aboard.

The Atlanta Police Department referred all inquiries to the FBI. Atlanta's FBI bureau confirmed it is looking into the matter, though no arrests have been made and agents did not meet the flight at the gate.

---

## Security Implications

The incident highlights the ongoing security challenges posed by in-flight Wi-Fi networks, which remain a attractive target for malicious actors due to the enclosed, high-value environment. Security researchers at DEF CON have long documented the vulnerabilities of aviation Wi-Fi systems, and the ability to jam onboard networks and broadcast substitute signals demonstrates a sophisticated level of technical knowledge.
