# AI Aging Clocks Reveal Donor Livers Can Be Made Biologically Younger

Researchers using AI-powered biological aging clocks have discovered that donated livers kept on perfusion machines are biologically younger than organs stored on ice — a finding that could expand the pool of viable donor organs and reduce transplant failures. The study, published September 14, 2026, provides molecular evidence for why perfused organs consistently outperform cold-stored organs in clinical outcomes.

---

## The Problem with Cold Storage

When a donor organ is removed from the body, the clock starts ticking. Surgeons typically flush the organ with preservative solution, bag it, and place it on ice — where it immediately begins to degrade. The standard window for transplantation is measured in hours.

For organs in suboptimal condition — from older donors or those with health issues — cold storage is particularly harsh. These organs often fail after transplant, or are deemed unsuitable and discarded altogether. Some hospitals have adopted perfusion machines that pump organs with nutrients and remove waste products, typically for six to twelve hours before transplant. Clinical studies have shown perfused organs tend to do better post-transplant, but the mechanism was not well understood.

---

## AI Aging Clocks Measure Molecular Age

A team at Mass General Brigham, including transplant surgeons Heidi Yeh and Alban Longchamp, used "aging clocks" — AI models trained on epigenetic patterns — to assess donated livers at the molecular level. These clocks measure chemical marks on DNA that accumulate with age and stress. A higher biological age in an organ correlates with worse outcomes after transplant.

The researchers compared tissue samples from 37 livers donated across 19 donors — some kept on ice, others maintained on perfusion machines. The difference was striking: perfused livers showed significantly lower biological ages, equivalent to being years younger at the molecular level than their cold-stored counterparts.

---

## Why This Matters for Transplant Medicine

The findings help explain a longstanding clinical puzzle: organs from younger donors consistently succeed more often than those from older donors, even when the recipients are similar. The aging clock provides a quantitative tool to assess organ health beyond donor age — potentially allowing surgeons to identify usable organs that would otherwise be discarded.

The research also validates the growing clinical use of perfusion machines. By keeping organs metabolically active rather than cold and dormant, these devices appear to reverse at least some of the damage that accumulates during retrieval and storage.

---

## Future Applications

The researchers are exploring whether aging clocks could be used to test organ health before transplant and to develop interventions that further reduce biological age. The goal is to increase the number of viable donor organs available each year — a persistent bottleneck in transplant medicine.
