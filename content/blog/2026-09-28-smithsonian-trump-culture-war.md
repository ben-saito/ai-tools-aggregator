# How the Smithsonian Became the Latest Front in Trump's Culture War

The Smithsonian Institution finds itself at the center of a political storm as the Trump administration applies pressure over exhibits perceived as ideologically biased, raising questions about institutional independence and the boundaries of historical interpretation.

---

## The Franklin Exhibit That Triggered Backlash

At the heart of the controversy is a now-closed exhibit that speculated Benjamin Franklin may have used enslaved people in his electrical research experiments. The exhibit, which closed in 2025 but remains accessible online, noted that while Franklin seldom identified specific individuals who assisted with his experiments, indentured servants and enslaved people in his household could have been involved.

This detail became a focal point in a 162-page White House report released in July, which accused the Smithsonian of succumbing to "ideological capture" and moving away from "straightforward historical education and scholarship toward an extreme political activism that seeks to transform our country."

---

## The Administration's Broadside

In March 2025, President Donald Trump signed an executive order declaring that the Smithsonian had succumbed to "divisive, race-centered ideology." The July 4 timing—coinciding with the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence—was deliberate, framing the report as part of a broader celebration of American history.

Two House committees held back-to-back hearings in July where Republican members criticized Smithsonian leadership. Meanwhile, warnings of deep budget cuts have intensified: the institution receives more than $1 billion annually from the federal government, representing 62% of its total funding.

In September, Smithsonian secretary Lonnie Bunch—the first African American to hold that position—announced his resignation, a departure that observers connected to the political pressure.

---

## Historical Context

The Smithsonian has historically maintained bipartisan support despite its role as the nation's premier cultural institution, drawing approximately 15 million visitors annually to its 21 museums. Previous controversies include the 1995 Enola Gay exhibit that generated backlash from veterans groups.

But the current confrontation represents an escalation. Unlike previous conflicts initiated by the administration, this battle has roots extending beyond Trump. Internal complaints about the direction of the National Museum of American History predate his return to office, reflecting longer-running tensions about how the institution should present American history.

---

## Funding Vulnerability

The Smithsonian's dependence on federal appropriations makes it structurally vulnerable to political pressure. Unlike institutions with diversified funding sources, the Smithsonian cannot easily offset congressional displeasure through private philanthropy alone.

Republican former congressman Garret Graves, who served on the Smithsonian board in 2023 and 2024, noted that board members had anticipated a reckoning. "I said over and over again that there's going to be a day of reckoning," he recalled. "You can either participate in it, help guide the Smithsonian back to something that makes more sense, or you can have an abrupt shift."

---

## The Fundamental Question

At stake is nothing less than how America tells its own story. The National Museum of American History has long grappled with tensions between technological achievement and social history, between celebrating national triumphs and acknowledging systemic failures.

Curators face pressure from multiple directions: donors concerned about critical treatments of American business, veterans groups sensitive to military history presentations, and an administration demanding a more celebratory narrative. The museum's lack of a unified approach has left it exposed to attack from all sides.

As the institution enters a new phase under likely leadership changes, the broader question of whether federal cultural institutions can maintain independence from political pressure remains acutely unresolved.
