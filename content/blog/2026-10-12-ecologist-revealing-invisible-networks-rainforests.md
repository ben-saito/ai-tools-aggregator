# The Ecologist Revealing Invisible Networks That Revive Rainforests

---

Daisy Dent has spent fifteen years documenting the underground networks that connect rainforest trees — fungal threads that shuttle nutrients, water, and chemical signals between plants sometimes hundreds of meters apart. Her work, combining field ecology with machine learning analysis of soil samples, is reshaping how scientists understand forest resilience and recovery after deforestation.

The networks, called mycorrhizal fungi, are not passive conduits. Dent's research shows they actively redistribute carbon and nitrogen based on signals from stressed trees — a kind of emergency relief system encoded in the forest's biology. Forests that retain these networks recover from clear-cutting years faster than those where the fungal mat was severed by heavy machinery.

Machine learning plays a central role in parsing the complexity. Dent's team at the University of Exeter trains models on soil metagenomics data to predict which tree-fungal combinations will survive in degraded patches. The algorithm identifies optimal planting schemes that accelerate network formation, reducing restoration timelines from decades to years.

The implications extend beyond ecology. Carbon credit markets are beginning to incorporate mycorrhizal network preservation into their metrics, recognizing that intact fungal mats store carbon more efficiently than isolated plantings. Several reforestation projects in Brazil and Indonesia have adopted Dent's network-aware planting protocols.

A key challenge is scale: restoring a forest's underground network requires understanding soil chemistry, fungal species distribution, and tree population genetics simultaneously. Dent's lab is developing a handheld sequencing device that could allow field workers to assess fungal health and prescribe restoration strategies on-site, cutting the need for laboratory analysis from weeks to hours.

The work exemplifies a broader shift in conservation: moving from simple planting metrics toward ecosystem-level intervention design informed by computational modeling. As climate change accelerates forest degradation, the speed and precision enabled by AI tools may determine which forests survive.
