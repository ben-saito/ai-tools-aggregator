# US-China AI Safety Alert Proposal Faces Expert Criticism Over Lack of Technical Depth

The Trump administration's proposal to establish an AI safety notification channel between the United States and China has drawn skepticism from experts who argue the plan lacks the technical expertise needed to meaningfully address AI risks.

---

## A Channel Without Engineers

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced this week that the US and China had agreed to explore a system allowing both nations to send alerts when AI systems pose threats or behave unpredictably. The announcement came ahead of a scheduled meeting between Trump and Xi Jinping, with AI expected to top the agenda.

But experts quickly pointed out a critical flaw: the proposed mechanism includes no technical specialists capable of evaluating AI safety risks.

"The scientific, technical term is BS," one source familiar with the plan told Ars Technica, describing the proposal as superficial. The channel would connect government officials with no AI expertise, rather than researchers or engineers who could assess the urgency of reported risks.

Sam Bresnick, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, noted that while an existing communication channel is better than none, the absence of technically qualified personnel undermines the mechanism's purpose.

---

## Export Controls Complicate Trust

The proposal faces additional headwinds from the broader US-China tech rivalry. The Trump administration has steadily expanded export controls blocking China's access to advanced AI chips and hardware. Two weeks ago, the Justice Department indicted six Chinese AI companies for allegedly stealing from US frontier labs—charges China has strongly disputed.

These actions have fueled skepticism that US-led safety discussions are genuine efforts to manage AI risks, rather than vehicles for maintaining American technological dominance.

"Whenever Trump or CEOs like Sam Altman advocate for American-led global AI standards, all China probably sees is the US wielding safety discussions as a way to lock in its advantage," Bresnick said.

---

## The "AI Race" Narrative

Critics argue the framing of AI development as a competitive "race" that one nation must "win" is counterproductive to genuine safety cooperation.

Alvin Wang Graylin, a digital fellow at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab who has advised governments on AI policy, said the US focus on frontier AI dominance obscures a separate competition for global market share. Chinese open-source models have rapidly gained ground internationally, with adoption accelerating across developing markets.

"US-China capability gaps at the frontiers of AI technology typically last only a few months," Graylin wrote in a September analysis for the Quincy Institute. "There is no competitive 'moat' that will secure a permanent advantage."

Graylin advocated for a fundamentally different approach: cooperation on shared risks including AI-enabled infrastructure attacks, biological weapons proliferation, and economic disruption. He suggested the US could ease tensions by narrowing export controls to genuine military threats and enacting domestic safety regulations.

---

## What Experts Actually Want

Representative Ro Khanna, the ranking Democrat on the House Select Committee on Strategic Competition Between the US and Chinese Communist Party, argued that talks between Trump and Xi should go further—pursuing international agreements banning recursive self-improving AI systems and establishing safeguards against AI-enabled biological and nuclear weapons development.

Graylin went further, arguing that meaningful cooperation requires acknowledging that AI safety cannot be achieved through competition.

"There is no way to compete yourself to a safer world," he told Ars Technica.
