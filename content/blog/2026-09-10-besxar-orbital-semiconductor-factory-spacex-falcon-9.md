# Besxar Bets on Orbital Semiconductor Manufacturing -- Strapping Advanced Chip Fabs onto SpaceX Falcon 9 Rockets

If you want to manufacture semiconductors in space and bring the products back to Earth, your options are limited: wait for a slot on the International Space Station, or partner with a handful of startups launching on dedicated rockets. Besxar, a stealth semiconductor startup, has chosen the latter -- and it is building its fabrication equipment directly onto SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket bodies.

The approach is unconventional, even by the standards of an industry that has seen remarkable architectural shifts in recent years. Rather than launching finished chip fabrication equipment to orbit, Besxar is engineering its production lines to be mounted structurally to the rocket's second stage, effectively turning the vehicle itself into part of the manufacturing infrastructure.

---

## Why Orbit for Chip Manufacturing?

The fundamental appeal of zero-gravity, high-vacuum environments for semiconductor production has been explored academically for decades. Certain deposition and etching processes used in chip manufacturing behave differently -- and in some cases more precisely -- when gravity is removed from the equation. Crystal growth for silicon wafers can achieve higher uniformity without convective flow interfering with dopant distribution. Thin-film deposition processes can achieve more uniform coatings without gravitational settling effects.

For advanced compound semiconductors -- materials like gallium nitride (GaN) and silicon carbide (SiC) used in power electronics and RF components -- the orbital environment may offer meaningful quality advantages. These materials are notoriously difficult to process on Earth due to their thermal properties and crystal structure sensitivities. A microgravity environment could potentially produce wafers with defect densities that are structurally impossible to achieve under standard atmospheric conditions.

The economics become viable when launch costs drop sufficiently -- which they have. SpaceX's Falcon 9 has driven the cost of delivering payload to low Earth orbit well below $3,000 per kilogram at scale. If the value of orbital-manufactured chips is high enough, the launch cost becomes a manageable line item rather than a prohibitive constraint.

## The Technical Architecture

Besxar's approach differs from earlier concepts like space-based solar power or orbital refineries in a critical way: it does not require the manufacturing equipment to remain in orbit permanently. The Falcon 9 second stage, with Besxar's fabrication modules attached, completes its manufacturing run during a standard mission window -- typically a multi-day orbital stay -- and returns to Earth intact for refurbishment and reflight.

This is architecturally significant. It means Besxar does not need to solve the hard problem of building a permanent orbital factory with all the life-support, thermal regulation, and debris mitigation that implies. The rocket itself provides structural integration, power, and thermal management during the manufacturing window. Return and landing happen under the same vehicle architecture SpaceX already operates.

The manufacturing cycle involves: module attachment to the second stage prior to launch; activation of deposition and growth processes once stable orbit is achieved; monitoring and control via telemetry during the orbital stay; deactivation and preparation for re-entry; and return to a designated landing zone for module recovery.

## The Business Model and Target Market

Besxar is not positioning itself as a volume manufacturer of commodity chips. Its target market is specialty semiconductor components where performance premiums justify orbital production costs: radiation-hardened chips for satellite and spacecraft electronics, where the natural radiation shielding of Earth's atmosphere is absent; high-performance RF components for next-generation wireless infrastructure; and compound semiconductor materials for electric vehicle power electronics where the performance delta over Earth-manufactured equivalents translates directly into system-level efficiency gains.

The company has not disclosed its valuation or total funding. TechCrunch reported in August 2026 that the startup was actively recruiting hardware and process engineers with experience in both orbital systems and semiconductor process technology -- a combination skill set that narrows the candidate pool considerably.

## Competitive Landscape and Execution Risks

Besxar is not the only company exploring non-traditional manufacturing environments for chips. Several aerospace and semiconductor companies have conducted microgravity experiments aboard the ISS and private orbital platforms. What distinguishes Besxar is the direct integration with an existing, operational launch vehicle -- a decision that trades schedule risk for architectural risk.

The execution challenges are substantial. Vibration loads during launch and re-entry impose mechanical stresses on fabrication equipment that do not exist in a ground-based fab. Thermal cycling across the range from sea level to orbital temperatures and back again places demanding cycle requirements on process chambers and material interfaces. The refurbishment and requalification process between flights adds turnaround time and cost that must be factored into the per-unit economics.

There is also regulatory exposure. The U.S. Department of Commerce maintains export controls on certain semiconductor manufacturing technologies, and the classification of orbital manufacturing as a controlled activity -- and whether the resulting products are subject to the same restrictions as Earth-manufactured equivalents -- remains an unsettled legal question that could affect Besxar's ability to serve certain customers and markets.

---

## What Happens Next

Besxar has not announced a first mission date. The company's hiring profile and supply chain activity suggest it is in the process of qualifying its first-generation fabrication modules for Falcon 9 integration. A precursor mission to demonstrate the core manufacturing processes in orbit -- likely carrying a simplified version of the full production stack -- would be a logical precursor to commercial operations.

For the semiconductor industry, the broader question is whether orbital manufacturing transitions from academic curiosity to commercial infrastructure. If Besxar's early missions demonstrate meaningful quality improvements for targeted compound semiconductor products, the economics of space manufacturing become significantly more attractive -- and other players will move to establish competing orbital production capabilities.

The intersection of the launch industry and the semiconductor industry has rarely been more active. SpaceX has already proven that radical cost reduction in access to orbit changes what is economically viable to manufacture off Earth. Whether that logic extends to semiconductor fabrication is the question Besxar is betting its entire company to answer.
