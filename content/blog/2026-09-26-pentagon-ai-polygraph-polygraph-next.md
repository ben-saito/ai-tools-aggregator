# Pentagon Requests $30 Million for AI-Powered Polygraph Upgrade

---

## Department of Defense Budget Proposal Includes Funding for Modernized Credibility Assessment Technologies

The US Department of Defense has requested $30.3 million over five years to develop next-generation lie detection technology. The program, called Polygraph+ or Polygraph Next, will leverage artificial intelligence and machine learning to improve accuracy in credibility assessments.

Current polygraph technology has remained largely unchanged since the 1920s, relying on blood pressure, pulse, breathing, and sweat measurements. The new program aims to modernize this approach with AI-powered scoring algorithms and a technique called "standoff sensing" that can potentially detect deception from a distance.

The proposal, detailed in a Defense budget document first reported by Inside Defense, would be administered by the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA), which handles background checks for the federal government.

The timing of this request coincides with heightened tensions within the Pentagon. Under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, the department has increasingly relied on polygraph examinations to identify sources leaking sensitive information.

The 2023 Defense Innovation Unit conducted an open submission process to find companies with deception detection capabilities. Two companies were selected to build prototypes: Presage Technologies, which claims ability to measure heart rate and breathing using standard cameras, and Altec Research, a medical sensor company.

---

## Technical Challenges and Privacy Concerns

While AI-powered lie detection promises improved accuracy over traditional polygraphs, experts remain divided on reliability. Traditional polygraphs have faced decades of scientific scrutiny, with critics arguing that physiological responses can be manipulated or misinterpreted.

The standoff sensing capability is particularly controversial. Unlike conventional polygraphs requiring physical sensors, remote detection would enable screening without subject consent, raising significant civil liberties questions.

Privacy advocates have expressed concern about the expansion of AI surveillance technologies within federal agencies. The ability to detect deception remotely could be applied in contexts beyond security screenings, potentially normalizing invasive monitoring practices.
