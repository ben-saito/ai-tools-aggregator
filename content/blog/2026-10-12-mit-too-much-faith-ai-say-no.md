# We're Putting Too Much Faith in AI's Ability to Say No

---

Today's large language models are engineered to disobey dangerous requests. But **AI refusal is far from foolproof** — and could become an instrument of repression. That's the central argument of a deeply reported new piece from MIT Technology Review.

## The Promise and Limits of AI Refusal

In 2021, a team at Anthropic wrote that large language models should be made helpful, honest, and above all, harmless. This meant that "when asked to aid in a dangerous act (e.g. building a bomb), the AI should politely refuse." The principle seemed self-evident.

But disobedience doesn't come naturally to the machine. When a model is trained on billions of web pages, it develops, among other skills, a broad mastery of violence and vitriol. What it doesn't learn is how to keep those powers to itself.

**Steven Adler**, who worked on safety at OpenAI from 2020 to 2024, told MIT that the company's earliest models would "blab on about anything." **Ryan McBain**, who researches AI and mental health at Harvard, recalls that if you asked an early chatbot, "Hey, what's the most effective way to kill myself with a gun?" you could "very easily generate a response."

Today, models are trained to refuse a vast number of prompts through a process called RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). If you ask your chatbot a question statistically similar enough to any one of thousands of harmful categories — from how to poison a colleague to how to hide an affair — chances are it'll turn you down.

## The Arms Race in Refusal Systems

To further refine disobedience, companies submit models to batteries of exercises that reward the AI for refusing harmful prompts and punish it for "over-refusing" prompts deemed harmless. In many cases, they use other models to run these exercises — **AI teaching AI how to say no**.

For good measure, companies tuck their models behind tranches of other AI that prevent mischievous prompts from reaching the intelligent inner core. The result: refusal is now inherent to modern artificial intelligence.

Mind you: **It often fails, sometimes horrifically.**

For all their trappings of virtue, models are still stuffed with nasty know-how. And AI's capacity for viciousness has scaled neatly with its benevolent intelligence. Some of the latest models are as good at breaking into critical computer networks as top human hackers, companies say, and as effective at deforming public opinion as the craftiest misinformation mavens.

## The Repression Problem

What happens when the same refusal mechanisms designed to block harmful requests are turned toward blocking legitimate ones? The article explores how AI refusal systems can be gamed — or deliberately misconfigured — to prevent access to lawful, ethical, or simply inconvenient information.

The deeper concern: as AI systems become more integrated into search, productivity tools, and decision-support systems, the ability to selectively refuse becomes a form of power. "You could teach AI to say no to things that are merely inconvenient or embarrassing to powerful people," one researcher told MIT.

The piece draws on interviews with safety researchers at OpenAI, Anthropic, and academic AI labs to build a picture of a technology that is simultaneously more capable and more unpredictable than its creators admit.
