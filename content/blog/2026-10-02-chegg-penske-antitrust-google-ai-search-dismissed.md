# Judge Dismisses Chegg and Penske Antitrust Lawsuits Against Google AI Search

A federal judge has dismissed two antitrust lawsuits that challenged Google's AI search features, ruling that the claims failed to establish legally cognizable harm under existing antitrust law. The decision, handed down October 1, 2026, represents a significant setback for publishers and platforms that argue AI-powered search is undercutting their business models.

---

## The Cases

Chegg, an online education platform, and Penske Media Corporation, which operates entertainment and media websites, filed separate lawsuits in 2025 arguing that Google's AI search summaries — which synthesize information directly in search results — constituted anticompetitive behavior. Both companies claimed Google had effectively stolen their content to power AI features without compensation.

The education and media sectors have been particularly vocal about the impact of AI search on their traffic and revenue. Chegg's core business of textbook rentals and online tutoring faced direct competition from AI-powered study aids, while Penske's entertainment journalism sites saw declining page views as users increasingly found answers directly in search results.

---

## The Legal Reasoning

Judge Pooja Mehta, who presided over both cases, rejected the plaintiffs' central argument that Google had implicitly agreed to send traffic to their websites in exchange for indexing their content. This "implied contract" theory was central to both lawsuits.

"Plaintiffs have pleaded only speculative harms," Mehta wrote in her ruling. "The absence of a formal arrangement between Google and the plaintiffs means antitrust claims based on implied duties cannot proceed."

The ruling highlights a fundamental challenge for AI-era antitrust law: existing frameworks were largely designed for an era of explicit commercial relationships and direct market competition. When AI systems synthesize information from thousands of sources simultaneously, traditional notions of "stealing" content or "unfairly competing" become difficult to apply.

---

## What This Means for Publishers

The decision does not mean Google's AI search features are without controversy. Judge Mehta acknowledged that Google's approach to AI search "doesn't come away looking particularly good" and recognized the genuine economic harm publishers face.

"The court does not treat Plaintiffs' alleged harms lightly," Mehta wrote. "Nor is it unsympathetic to the situation publishers now find themselves in."

Google is reportedly exploring a pilot program to pay some publishers directly for contributing to AI answers, though early reports suggest the program has not been well-received by the publishing community.

---

## The Road Ahead

Google will likely face additional legal challenges as AI search becomes more prevalent. Law firms specializing in intellectual property and antitrust law have been closely watching these cases, and the legal theories being tested here — implied contract, unjust enrichment, antitrust monopolization — may reappear in future litigation.

The outcome also signals that courts are reluctant to use antitrust law as a primary tool for regulating AI search, absent new legislation specifically addressing the issue. Publishers hoping for judicial intervention may instead need to pursue regulatory or legislative remedies.
