# Welcome to the Spiderverse: Spiderwebs as Biodiversity Monitoring Tools

Researchers are discovering that spiderwebs are an unexpectedly powerful tool for monitoring biodiversity and tracking environmental changes. By analyzing the DNA trapped in spider silk — known as environmental DNA or eDNA — scientists can identify the wide variety of organisms that live near the web, from the spider's prey to nearby plants and animals. A new study from Curtin University in Australia demonstrates that spiderwebs outperform all other passive sampling methods for detecting vertebrate species, making them a cheap and scalable alternative to traditional wildlife surveys.

---

## Spider Silk as Nature's DNA Collection System

Spider silk is extraordinarily sticky, designed to ensnare insects and other prey. But researchers have found that the same property makes it effective at capturing genetic material shed by all kinds of organisms in the vicinity. Every living thing — from mammals to insects to plants — constantly sheds DNA into the environment, and spider silk readily traps this material.

Joshua Newton, a molecular ecologist at Curtin University in Perth, Australia, has been sampling a wide range of environmental substrates to monitor wildlife populations. His work includes vegetation swabs, water samples, soil cores, and even air filters on moving vehicles. But spiderwebs stand out. "It's probably one of the best substrates we've used and tried to date," Newton says, noting that webs consistently captured more vertebrate DNA than any other passive sampling method in his comparative study.

In research conducted near a zoo and wildlife sanctuary outside Perth, Newton found that spiderwebs detected more vertebrate species than vegetation swabs, water samples, or fan-based air filters. The results echo findings from a similar study conducted in France, confirming that webs have unique advantages as a biodiversity sampling tool.

---

## The Catch: Destroying 40 Webs for Data

The main limitation of using real spiderwebs for eDNA analysis is that collecting meaningful data requires destroying approximately 40 webs per sampling site. "It kind of goes against everything that we're trying to do if we're collecting the home of an organism," Newton notes — a significant concern for researchers studying ecosystems that include spiders as key predators.

This problem caught the attention of Angela McGaughran, a genomicist at New Zealand's University of Waikato. She devised a practical solution: synthetic spiderwebs. McGaughran purchased faux webs from a Halloween store, wrapped them around coat hangers, and hung them outside in natural settings.

The synthetic material performed remarkably well. It successfully captured eDNA from nearby cows and sheep, exotic birds at a nearby aviary, and — notably — detected fungal spores even more effectively than real webs. This last finding is particularly significant for plant pathologists monitoring agricultural threats, as early detection of fungal diseases can prevent widespread crop failures.

---

## Advantages Over Traditional Biodiversity Monitoring

Traditional wildlife surveys require significant human labor — trained observers must trap, count, or otherwise directly detect organisms. This process is time-consuming, expensive, and can miss species that are rare, nocturnal, or otherwise difficult to detect. eDNA approaches using spiderwebs offer several advantages:

**Scalability**: Faux spiderwebs can be deployed in large numbers across wide geographic areas at relatively low cost, enabling monitoring at scales impractical for traditional surveys.

**Non-invasive to target species**: Unlike trapping or direct observation, web sampling does not require capturing or disturbing the animals themselves.

**Broad taxonomic coverage**: A single web can capture DNA from dozens of species simultaneously, from insects to mammals to plants.

**Continuous sampling**: Webs accumulate eDNA over time, providing an integrated picture of species presence during the deployment period rather than a snapshot at a single moment.

---

## Future Directions: Scaling Up with Artificial Webs

McGaughran's group is now developing larger experiments to test whether artificial spiderwebs can unlock a cheap and scalable way to monitor biodiversity across agricultural and natural landscapes. The approach could be particularly valuable for conservation efforts in regions where establishing traditional survey infrastructure is difficult or prohibitively expensive.

Researchers are also exploring how different web designs, materials, and deployment strategies affect eDNA capture efficiency. The goal is to develop standardized protocols that researchers and conservation practitioners can adopt without specialized equipment or expertise.

The spiderweb-to-biodiversity-monitoring pipeline represents a striking example of how fundamental biological research — in this case, understanding how spider silk works — can yield practical tools for environmental science and conservation.
