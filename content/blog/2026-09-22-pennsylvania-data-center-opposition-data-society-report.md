# Data & Society report: Pennsylvania communities push back against AI data center boom

---

## The social cost of AI infrastructure

A new report from the nonprofit Data & Society offers the most detailed ethnographic account yet of why communities in Pennsylvania have mobilized against AI data center construction. The study, based on 18 months of fieldwork and interviews with 44 Pennsylvanians between 2024 and 2026, documents a remarkable shift: data center projects worth $68 billion were disrupted by local opponents in recent years, with opposition crossing traditional political lines.

The researchers describe the opposition as "post-partisan" — not a left-right divide but a coalition united by concerns about property values, electricity costs, and the secretive way data center developers approach community meetings. "There is a significant group of people who are just really clued into their town hall meetings and might notice that wow, a representative from Amazon, Meta, Microsoft showed up here, or a data center developer showed up at my community meeting — that's a little odd," said Maia Woluchem, a program director at Data & Society who co-authored the report.

---

## From economic promise to community resistance

When the researchers began their work, data centers were being presented as a key to economic development. Governor Josh Shapiro headlined a July 2025 summit celebrating $90 billion in data center and AI infrastructure investment. A year later, Shapiro signed an order imposing new requirements on data center projects near residential areas — a policy reversal that reflects how quickly the political calculus around AI infrastructure has changed.

The report documents how AI companies and their investors struggle to reconcile growing demand for AI products with opposition to the infrastructure behind them. Industry narratives frame AI as "inevitable" — a technological tide that communities should simply accept. But Pennsylvania residents, the researchers found, have deep personal experience with industrial disruption: coal mining, early oil industry, steel manufacturing, and fracking have all left lasting trauma in the state's communities. "People in Pennsylvania especially have incredible experience with industrial change, have deep lineages of industrial trauma, enough to sniff when things don't feel right to them," Woluchem said.

---

## The industry's response: coalitions, lobbying, and foreign influence claims

The Data & Society researchers were surprised by the diversity of opposition. The most counterintuitive finding: even trade unions, typically supportive of infrastructure projects for the jobs they bring, have become a major source of resistance — not because they oppose data centers outright, but because they are skeptical of industry promises about employment. Once built, data centers employ relatively few workers. Union leaders see the projects as a way to rebuild ranks through construction jobs, but many members are not convinced the long-term benefits will materialize.

In contrast to industry fears about foreign interference in data center debates — a narrative promoted by some tech companies suggesting that opposition is astroturfed by adversarial states — the researchers describe the industry's own public relations effort as a kind of propaganda campaign. The Data Center Coalition, a trade group backed by major tech companies, has operated websites like "Pennsylvania Connects" to promote projects and spent at least $19,000 on lobbying activities.

---

## What people are actually worried about

The report identifies three concrete concerns driving opposition. First, residents worry that data center electrical usage will increase their own power bills — a legitimate concern given that data centers are extraordinarily power-intensive and grid operators have issued warnings about capacity in some regions. Second, property owners fear that large data center facilities will depress residential property values in surrounding neighborhoods. Third, many Pennsylvanians are upset by the tactics data center developers use to secure approvals — showing up at community meetings without adequate notice, holding sessions at odd hours, and engaging in what locals describe as aggressive persuasion campaigns.

"One of the strengths of ethnography and doing research on the ground for an extended period of time is piercing the bubble of AI hype," said Livia Garofalo, a Data & Society researcher. "Communities aren't opposed to technology in the abstract — they're making very specific judgments about what is being built in their backyards, by whom, and on whose terms."

---

## The broader implications for AI deployment

The Pennsylvania case has become a focal point for the broader debate over how AI infrastructure will be deployed at scale. Survey data cited in the report shows that more than 60% of Americans favor limiting new data centers, particularly in their own communities. That sentiment is growing, not shrinking, as more communities become aware of what data center construction actually entails.

For AI companies, the challenge is not merely technical or financial — it is political. Building the data centers needed to power the next generation of AI models requires navigating local opposition, winning over skeptical regulators, and addressing legitimate concerns about energy consumption and community impact. The Data & Society report suggests that the industry's current approach — framing opposition as irrational or externally orchestrated — is unlikely to succeed. The communities on the front lines of this debate are paying close attention to what is actually being built, by whom, and what it will mean for their neighborhoods.
