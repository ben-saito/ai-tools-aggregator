# Bill Gates AI Danger Thresholds Essay: "We've Crossed the Threshold"

---

## AI Danger Thresholds: Gates Sounds Alarm on Bio, Cyber, and Labor Impacts

Bill Gates, the former Microsoft CEO and philanthropist, published a sweeping essay on August 26, 2026, arguing that multiple AI danger thresholds have already been crossed, and that society is not paying sufficient attention. In an interview with MIT Technology Review, Gates said: "We've crossed the threshold in terms of [AI's] bio-capabilities, cyber-capabilities, psychosocial capabilities, job-market-destruction capabilities, and even the lack of control."

The essay is the first in a planned series where Gates intends to lay out what he sees as the most pressing risks of frontier AI systems.

---

## Bioterrorism Risk: 50 Times More Likely Than Natural Pandemic

Among Gates's most striking warnings are his concerns about the bio-capabilities of current frontier models. "Any model that can make novel molecules should be monitored," he said. "I view bioterrorism risk, versus a natural pandemic, as about 50 times more scary, more likely than a natural pandemic risk."

Gates is calling for international monitoring standards for AI systems capable of generating novel molecular structures, and suggests the US should engage China on establishing shared standards for biomonitoring of AI models.

---

## Cyberattack Threshold Already Crossed

Gates argues that the threshold for non-technical individuals to conduct sophisticated cyberattacks using AI has already been passed. "We're there!" he stated in the interview, expressing frustration that this capability exists without meaningful public discussion or policy response.

He criticized the notion that protests against data center construction are an effective response: "You can stop every data center in the United States and it won't change any of the issues that I'm talking about. Data centers will be built globally."

---

## Robot Tax and Human-Reserved Jobs: Policy Proposals

As novel policy responses, Gates advances two main proposals. The first is a robot and token tax — a value-added tax on AI usage that would fund a safety net for workers displaced by AI. "A token tax is a sales tax, value-added tax, vertically oriented like an alcohol, tobacco, or luxury-type tax," he explained.

The second is the concept of "human-reserved jobs" — societally designated roles that would be preserved for human workers, potentially varying by country. "Childcare," Gates noted as an example. He acknowledged the difficulty of achieving international consensus, and suggested that countries could use trade policy mechanisms similar to the EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) to enforce human-reservation standards.

---

## Why Self-Regulation Cannot Work

Gates was blunt about the limitations of industry self-regulation. "You can't count on an industry to self-regulate. You can't," he said. "It's kind of a crazy idea."

He described private conversations where executives at AI companies express concern, but noted that competitive dynamics and the need to raise capital make genuine restraint unlikely. "The industry doesn't like criticizing itself, or players like criticizing each other," he said. "Some companies are hiring fewer entry-level workers, which you'd have to call a pretty modest signal."

On the prospect of getting AI companies to voluntarily slow down development, Gates said: "If it was realistic to get people to slow down, you would be advocating for them to slow down." He sees this as politically infeasible.

---

## The Pace of Change vs. Historical Precedent

Gates pushed back on comparisons to past technological revolutions. "The past definitely is very misleading on this, and a lot of people lean on that. 'Hey, no previous technology resulted in a net jobs reduction,' and they're right. And I've given that speech."

He argued that when AI can replace human cognition across most industries simultaneously, at modest cost relative to human labor, and with error rates potentially lower than human rates, historical precedent becomes inapplicable. "The current economic statistics are very misleading," he said.

On the timeline, Gates said the transition could happen faster than previous revolutions: "Not in any long time frame — because the things that hold people back in terms of capabilities and reliability, all those things are being solved."

---

## AI Agents and the Future of Work

Gates outlined a world where most well-defined jobs are immediately vulnerable to AI replacement. "Any job that's well defined, the AI is cheaper and better," he said. "Yes, people have seen cases where it was implemented wrong. The data wasn't right. So, say it takes a couple years for people to realize that such a high percentage of white-collar jobs are achievable by paying an AI a lot less money."

He noted robotics progress as particularly rapid: "We have crossed the threshold that for a substantial part of the white-collar market, you have very low-cost substitution. And then you can have an opinion on how quickly robotics come along. We're not there yet, but it is stunning the progress being made there — a little bit more in China than in the US, but somewhat in both."

He predicted that once robots are capable enough for factory work, the threshold for broader deployment would be crossed nearly simultaneously: "As soon as the robot's good enough to work in a factory, it's probably good enough to cook food, clean rooms, go to construction sites, take all the warehouse jobs. You cross the threshold, and boom, that's almost 30% of the job market."

---

## Optimism About Long-Term Abundance, Pessimism About Transition

Despite the stark warnings, Gates expressed optimism about the long-term destination: "Eventually you have abundance." He pointed to AI's potential in healthcare, agriculture, education, and bureaucracy navigation as genuinely transformative positive applications.

But the transition, he warned, will be turbulent. "We've got to get through a very tumultuous period," Gates said. "You've got winners and losers at this point. Houses are not going to get cheap really quickly. Education, because of the way we think of it as credential, it's not going to get cheap really quickly."

He also discussed the mental health AI company Limbic, noting that patients sometimes prefer AI interactions, and the nursing AI Hippocratic as examples of AI systems that can operate at scale with perfect memory and availability.

---

## The Limits of His Own Credibility

Asked whether he can still be an effective messenger given controversies and conspiracies associated with his name, Gates was pragmatic. "I'm an imperfect messenger," he acknowledged. "You've got to really, right now, you've got to understand the technology and the slope it's on, and you have to know something about cyber or bio or psychosocial. People should be able to get that. I don't know why they're not more concerned."

He dismissed legacy concerns and expressed willingness to work with any political leader to advance AI safety, though he noted his primary focus in recent years has been global health and poverty reduction through the Gates Foundation.

---

## The Path Forward: Government Expertise and International Cooperation

Gates called for dramatically increasing AI expertise within government, noting that unlike previous technological revolutions, governments are not the leading-edge buyers or R&D funders in AI. "The depth of knowledge in the government isn't necessarily super-strong, because they are not the cutting-edge buyer or even the big R&D funder," he said.

He advocated for renewed US-China cooperation on AI safety standards, calling it "by far the most important" area for bilateral engagement. "You have to show what you're willing to do domestically. You don't even have to do it. You have to say what you're planning to do — and then I have no reason to think the Chinese won't go along, that models that create the molecules have to be monitored."
