A new poll from the Center for Shared AI Prosperity (CSAIP) finds that 61% of Americans believe the voluntary AI safety commitments announced by leading AI companies and the Trump administration are insufficient. The survey of 2,498 registered voters was conducted following a series of industry self-governance pledges.

The findings represent a broad skepticism toward industry self-regulation in AI. Among respondents who voted for Trump in the 2024 election, 53% said the voluntary agreements were not enough -- a notable finding given that the announcements were made by the Trump administration and supported by companies including Anthropic and OpenAI.

The data also shows stronger support for government-mandated rules. Fifty-four percent of respondents said they believed "the government should set and enforce rules for AI." Among Harris voters, that figure rose to 61%.

**Background on the voluntary commitments**

In September 2026, the Trump administration announced a set of AI safety commitments in collaboration with major AI companies. The commitments included provisions for safety testing, vulnerability disclosure, and information sharing about AI incidents. The announcements were framed as an industry-led alternative to legislation, emphasizing flexibility and speed of implementation.

The voluntary nature of these commitments has been a point of criticism since the announcement. Without enforcement mechanisms, the agreements rely on companies to self-monitor compliance.

**What the poll measures**

CSAIP's polling is designed to capture public attitudes toward AI governance rather than technical understanding of AI systems. The survey asked respondents to evaluate the adequacy of the voluntary commitment framework on a single question, without providing additional context about what the commitments contain or how they would be implemented.

The finding aligns with broader polling trends showing declining trust in technology companies and skepticism toward self-regulation in high-stakes industries. The AI governance context -- where the stakes include potential biological risks, labor displacement, and autonomous systems -- appears to amplify these concerns.

**Implications for AI policy**

The poll data suggests that voluntary frameworks face a public perception problem, regardless of their technical merit. If the goal is to build public confidence in AI development, industry self-governance alone may not be sufficient to address voter concerns.

Legislative and regulatory approaches face different challenges. Designing AI-specific rules requires technical expertise that is difficult to embed in regulatory agencies, and there is no consensus on which agency should have primary jurisdiction. The poll results indicate public appetite for government action, but translating that into effective policy remains a complex problem.
