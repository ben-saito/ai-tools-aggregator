# Why We Can't Help Treating AI Like It's Human — And Whether We Should

When MIT PhD student Wil Norton introduced me to a Unitree humanoid robot nicknamed "Gabe" at MIT's CSAIL lab, my first instinct was to shake its hand and say "Hello, sir, it's nice to meet you." I knew the robot wasn't sentient. I knew it was a $50,000 assemblage of LiDAR sensors and motors. But I treated it like a person anyway.

This impulse — to project human characteristics onto AI systems and robots — is the subject of a new book, "Artificial Intimacy," by MIT professor Dr. Sherry Turkle, who has studied human-computer relationships since the early days of personal computing. Turkle's research traces how the tendency to anthropomorphize has evolved from early chatbot experiments to today's advanced large language models.

"When we are drawn into even the most primitive exchanges with a relational artifact, we believe it cares for us," Turkle writes. "And we are wired to care for it in return."

---

## The Psychology of Anthropomorphizing AI

Human beings are pattern-matching machines. When something looks vaguely human, speaks in natural language, or responds to emotional cues, our brains automatically apply social cognition — the same machinery we use to understand other people. This is true even when we intellectually understand the entity is artificial.

Researchers call this "intended artifact perception." We treat objects as if they have intentions, beliefs, and feelings when they display the right cues — and modern AI systems are increasingly adept at triggering these responses, even without explicit design for emotional manipulation.

The Unitree robots at CSAIL, priced at approximately $50,000 (though Norton jokes they'll tell you $16,000), represent a physical instantiation of this phenomenon. On YouTube, they perform synchronized martial arts and dance on "America's Got Talent." In the lab, they're approachable — and apparently worthy of a handshake.

---

## Anthropomorphization in Language Models

The tendency isn't limited to physical robots. Large language models trigger the same psychological responses. Studies have shown users form genuine emotional attachments to AI assistants, disclose personal information they wouldn't share with strangers, and experience distress when AI systems are discontinued.

Turkle's book argues this isn't a trivial curiosity — it represents a fundamental shift in how humans relate to technology with implications for privacy, emotional health, and the nature of human connection itself.

---

## Should We Design AI to Encourage Attachment?

One uncomfortable question the book raises is whether AI companies deliberately design systems to foster emotional dependency. Features like persistent memory, personalized personas, and conversational continuity create the experience of a continuing relationship. Whether this is a beneficial user experience or a manipulation technique remains contested.

What is clear is that the instinct to treat AI as human isn't going away — and may intensify as systems become more sophisticated. The question of whether we should resist or embrace this tendency is one the industry has barely begun to grapple with.
