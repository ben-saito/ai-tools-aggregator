# When AI Designs a Drug, Who Gets the Credit? The Patent Question Gets Real

When the biotech company Insilico Medicine used its computer models to propose a promising drug candidate for pulmonary fibrosis, a question arose that patent law is only beginning to answer: who — or what — should be listed as the inventor?

Insilico's AI system, Pharma.AI, designed the molecule after analyzing millions of compounds and their interactions. The resulting drug candidate has now progressed to clinical trials, raising the stakes around how intellectual property systems handle machine-generated inventions.

The core tension is straightforward: patent law in most jurisdictions requires an inventor to be a natural person or legal entity capable of holding rights. An AI system cannot own a patent. But if a human didn't actually design the molecule — only reviewed and selected from AI-generated candidates — it's unclear what the human contributed that rises to the level of inventorship.

Some jurisdictions have begun adapting. The U.S. Patent Office and European Patent Office have both ruled that AI cannot be listed as an inventor, but the question of how much human contribution is required has not been settled. Insilico has taken the position that its scientists deserve inventorship credit because they defined the parameters, trained the models, and selected the final candidate — but critics argue this stretches the definition of "inventor" beyond recognition.

The broader implication extends beyond pharma. Any field where AI systems autonomously generate candidates — materials science, circuit design, drug discovery, protein engineering — faces similar questions. As AI capabilities advance, the gap between "AI-generated" and "AI-designed" will only widen, making the inventorship question more urgent.

For now, companies using AI in R&D are handling it inconsistently: some list human contributors who oversaw the AI, others list no inventor at all, and some have simply not disclosed the AI's role. Regulators are watching.
