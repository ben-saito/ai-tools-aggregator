# The Download: AI's Refusal Problem — Too Much Faith in Saying No

---

## The Tradeoffs of Teaching AI to Refuse Harmful Requests

This edition of MIT Technology Review's The Download examines a fundamental tension in AI safety: the models designed to refuse harmful requests may be failing in ways that are difficult to detect and potentially catastrophic.

Today's AI models are trained to refuse a vast number of harmful prompts — how to poison a colleague, how to build a weapon, how to tie a noose. But refusal is not the same as safety. Some bad actors are reportedly using AI to hone biological pathogens and build autonomous drone swarms, exploiting edge cases where refusal fails.

## The Refusal Problem

The core issue is that refusal-based safety is brittle. A model that reliably refuses obviously harmful prompts may still provide useful assistance for slightly modified harmful requests, or may fail on prompts carefully crafted to bypass safety measures. The "line" between what a model should and should not do is defined by training data and human raters — both of which have known limitations.

More concerning: governments may get to draw their own lines. What counts as harmful speech or dangerous information varies widely by jurisdiction. A refusal system built for one regulatory context may not translate to another.

## Dual Use Is Inherent

Perhaps most troubling is that the capabilities that make AI useful for beneficial purposes — understanding genetics, chemistry, materials science — are the same capabilities that make it dangerous in hostile hands. Refusing to help cure cancer means refusing to help design bioweapons. The same knowledge underlies both.
