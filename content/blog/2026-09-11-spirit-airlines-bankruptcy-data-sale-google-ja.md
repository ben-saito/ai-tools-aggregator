# Spirit航空破产→データ販売に懸念 -- 第三者の知的財産がGoogleに流出する恐れ

Spirit Airlines' bankruptcy filing has raised concerns about the sale of its data assets to Google, with one startup -- Springshot -- objecting that the deal could improperly transfer third-party intellectual property to the tech giant.

## Springshot's Objection

Springshot CEO Marcus Kreuzkamp told Ars Technica that his company powered Spirit's technology stack for the past three years, right up to the airline's last flight. Springshot provides a widely-used airline operations platform that helps both humans and AI systems improve efficiency and solve logistics problems quickly. Hundreds of airports globally use the platform.

Despite being a critical technology partner, Springshot received no advance notice when Spirit prepared to auction off a massive dataset in bankruptcy proceedings. Kreuzkamp is concerned that the dataset includes substantial data and IP that Springshot owns -- not Spirit.

## "Bankruptcy Cannot Become the New Land Grab for AI"

In a limited objection filed last month, Springshot argued that Spirit's sale agreement does not clearly define what data is being sold. The agreement vaguely references categories including "productivity and collaboration data," "core business systems and business application data," and "workflow and process data" -- categories that could sweep in Springshot's proprietary technology.

"This expansive definition does nothing to differentiate between Springshot's intellectual property that exists within Spirit's data repositories and systems, but Spirit does not own, and Spirit data that it actually owns and has the capacity to sell," Springshot argued in its court filing.

Kreuzkamp told Ars: "Bankruptcy cannot become the new land grab for AI." The statement reflects growing industry concern that bankrupt companies could become a backdoor for AI companies to acquire troves of proprietary data at distressed prices.

## Court Proceedings

Springshot has urged the court to pause Spirit's data sale until a transparent forensic process establishes that none of the data Google would acquire is actually owned by third parties. The case highlights a new legal frontier: how bankruptcy law applies to AI-era data assets, where the boundaries between vendor data, proprietary algorithms, and operator data are often unclear.

The outcome could set precedent for how courts handle data asset sales in bankruptcy proceedings, particularly when AI companies stand to acquire training data or proprietary business process information from insolvent competitors.
