# Nvidia's Les Karpas: Why Robots Are Waiting for Their ChatGPT Moment

The robotics industry has achieved remarkable things in controlled environments, but the breakthrough into everyday life has remained elusive. At TechCrunch Disrupt 2026, Nvidia's Les Karpas laid out his case for why that gap persists — and what it will take to close it.

---

## The Valley of Death for Robotics

Karpas argued that the robotics sector has been stuck in a familiar pattern: impressive demos that fail to translate into mass-market products. "The robotics industry is still waiting for their breakthrough into day-to-day life," he said at the conference.

The core problem, as Karpas sees it, is not hardware — the sensors and actuators exist — but the intelligence layer. Robot demos work in controlled labs because the world can be carefully scripted. The real world is messier, and current robots lack the generalization capability to handle that mess.

This mirrors a pattern already played out in AI: early rule-based systems worked in narrow domains until the shift to large pretrained models enabled the kind of broad generalization that launched products like ChatGPT. Karpas drew a direct line between the pre-ChatGPT era in AI and where robotics stands today.

---

## What Robots Need from Foundation Models

The ChatGPT moment for robots requires a few things to fall into place simultaneously, Karpas suggested. First, a foundation model trained on massive robotic data that captures the diversity of physical manipulation tasks. Second, a deployment stack that can run inference at the low latencies real-time control demands. Third, an ecosystem of hardware platforms that can host that software at price points viable for volume markets.

Nvidia has been building toward this on the compute side with its Jetson platform and the Isaac robotics stack. But Karpas was careful to frame this as an industry-wide challenge, not a problem Nvidia alone can solve.

---

## TechCrunch Disrupt 2026 as a Bellwether

The fact that Karpas chose TechCrunch Disrupt 2026 to lay out this argument is notable. Disrupt has become a venue where the robotics and AI communities cross-pollinate — startups, researchers, and incumbents all in the same hall.

The robotics sector has seen a wave of recent investment, with Figure, 1X Technologies, and Agility Robotics all raising substantial rounds. But Karpas's framing suggests that capital alone is not the bottleneck — the missing piece is the equivalent of a pretrained foundation model for physical intelligence.

Whether that model comes from a startup, a major lab like Nvidia's, or an unexpected source remains an open question. What seems clear from Karpas's remarks is that the robotics field is watching the AI playbook closely, and waiting for the moment when the next architecture shift makes the leap from lab to living room inevitable rather than aspirational.
