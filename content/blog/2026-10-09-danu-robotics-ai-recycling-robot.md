# Danu Robotics' Fight to Build a Better Recycling Robot

---

Amy Ma spent years working as a software developer at a London bank, watching recycling bins from multiple bins end up in the same trash truck. The economics of recycling often depend on humans manually sorting waste — a labor-intensive process with low margins and high error rates. Now she's building a robot to do it better.

Edinburgh-based **Danu Robotics** has developed H.E.R.O., a robotic sorting system that uses a pincer claw instead of the suction-based arms common in competitors like Glacier and RecycleEye. The key differentiator: Danu leverages **AI** to continuously improve the robot's software, learning from each sorting operation to boost accuracy and speed over time.

## The Business Case

Danu estimates that a single working site will earn $485,000 in additional revenue from having H.E.R.O. on the sorting line, against a $160,000 initial investment and $24,000 in annual maintenance. That's significantly cheaper than existing solutions, and Ma believes the robot will be more efficient to boot.

The company has signed $500,000 in contracts and has over 200 customers in its sales pipeline, targeting the $20 billion recycling sorting market across Europe and North America. With letters of intent from two large customers, Danu is preparing to scale.

## $5M Late-Seed to Scale

To fund commercial expansion, Danu Robotics has raised $5 million in late-seed funding. The company will use the capital to make the robot sturdier, smaller, and more versatile — with a goal of making it portable enough for use outside dedicated waste facilities.

"If it's smaller, it can be used as a stand-alone recycling solution for events, shopping centers, hospitals, or airports to recycle their waste at the source," Ma told TechCrunch. "That's the way we can really change how we're managing our packaging waste."

## AI-Powered Continuous Improvement

Unlike traditional robotic systems that require manual reprogramming for new sorting tasks, Danu's AI approach means the robot adapts. As it processes more materials, its neural models improve at identifying and categorizing different types of recyclables — reducing contamination rates that plague many recycling operations.

The company is participating in TechCrunch Disrupt's Startup Battlefield 200, October 13-15 in San Francisco.
