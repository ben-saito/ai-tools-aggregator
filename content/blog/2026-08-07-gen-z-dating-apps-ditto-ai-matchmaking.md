# Gen Z Dating Apps Like Ditto Ditch Swiping in Favor of AI Matchmaking

Gen Z is abandoning swipe-based dating apps in record numbers, turning instead to AI-powered matchmaking platforms that promise deeper connections without the endless scrolling. A new crop of startups—led by Ditto, founded by former UC Berkeley students Allen Wang and Eric Liu—is betting that machine learning can solve the loneliness epidemic better than Tinder's gamified approach ever could.

---

## The Problem with Swipe Culture

The numbers are bleak. The average Tinder user spends roughly 90 minutes per day swiping, yet match rates have plummeted as dating app fatigue sets in. A 2025 Pew Research study found that 64% of Americans under 35 who have used dating apps report negative experiences—citing superficiality, burnout, and a constant sense of being evaluated as a product rather than a person.

Swipe apps are optimized for engagement metrics, not romantic outcomes. The dopamine hit of a match competes with the slow, intentional work of building genuine relationships. Gen Z, having grown up watching their older siblings and parents divorce at record rates, appears to be rejecting this paradigm entirely.

---

## How AI Matchmaking Works

AI matchmaking platforms like Ditto move away from the swipe interface entirely. Instead, users engage in conversations with an AI that learns their communication style, values, and relationship goals over time. The system analyzes patterns across millions of successful relationships to identify compatibility—not just on surface-level interests, but on deeper dimensions like conflict resolution styles, life trajectory alignment, and emotional availability.

The shift represents a fundamental change in how dating technology is designed. Traditional apps treat users as passive content to be sorted; AI matchmaking treats users as active participants in a dynamic process of self-discovery.

---

## The Business of AI Love

The timing is commercially favorable. The global online dating market, valued at roughly $12 billion in 2025, is projected to grow at 8% annually through 2035. But the competitive landscape is shifting: established players like Hinge and Bumble are racing to integrate AI features, while well-funded startups like Ditto argue that incumbents are too structurally constrained to deliver truly intelligent matchmaking.

Ditto has reportedly raised a seed round from prominent venture investors, though the company has not disclosed terms. The startup's pitch is straightforward: existing apps monetize attention, not outcomes. A subscription model aligned with successful relationships—in which users pay when they find genuine compatibility—would fundamentally realign incentives.

---

## Risks and Ethical Questions

The AI matchmaking approach is not without controversy. Critics argue that algorithmically mediated relationships risk reducing human connection to optimization problems, potentially compounding loneliness if users defer too heavily to machine recommendations. There are also concerns about data privacy: intimate conversations with an AI dating coach create deeply sensitive datasets that, if breached or misused, could cause serious harm.

Regulatory frameworks for AI in romantic contexts remain nascent. The EU AI Act's provisions on high-risk AI systems do not explicitly cover dating platforms, though that may change as the technology matures.

---

## Looking Ahead

Whether AI can successfully address what psychologists call the "attachment crisis" in modern dating remains an open question. What is clear is that the market is moving: Gen Z's rejection of swipe culture has created a genuine opening for alternatives, and AI matchmaking is the most well-funded bet on what comes next.
