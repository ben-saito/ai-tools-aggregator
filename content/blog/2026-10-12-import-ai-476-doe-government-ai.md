# How the US Department of Energy Is Quietly Becoming a Major AI Actor

---

Two recent AI training projects conducted across US national laboratories are revealing how the Department of Energy is emerging as a significant player in the AI development ecosystem. The projects — one involving tri-labatory training across Sandia, Lawrence Livermore, and Los Alamos National Laboratories, and another utilizing NNSA supercomputing infrastructure — demonstrate that the US government is developing both the technical infrastructure and the institutional knowledge to train large-scale AI models at a scale that rivals major private labs.

---

## The Tri-Laboratory Training Approach

The first project involved coordinated training across three of the DOE's national laboratories: Sandia National Laboratories, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, and Los Alamos National Laboratory. Rather than training a single model on a single machine, the project trained a model using distributed compute across all three sites simultaneously — a significant engineering achievement that required solving coordination problems in federated large-scale training.

The tri-lab approach matters for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that the DOE's collection of supercomputers — spread across multiple sites with different specialties and different security profiles — can be treated as a unified compute resource for AI training. Second, it shows that the national labs have developed the software infrastructure to coordinate distributed training at scale, which is non-trivial. Third, it suggests a model for how government compute resources could be pooled for AI development in cases where national security interests justify the investment.

---

## NNSA Supercomputing for AI Training

The second project utilized infrastructure associated with the National Nuclear Security Administration — the branch of the DOE responsible for maintaining the US nuclear stockpile. NNSA supercomputers are among the most powerful in the world, purpose-built for the kind of high-fidelity physics simulation that nuclear weapons design requires. Using them for AI training is a significant signal about how seriously the government takes the AI competition.

The NNSA project trained a model using multiple supercomputers at multiple national labs, in what appears to be a rehearsal for larger-scale pooled compute initiatives. The explicit goal seems to be developing the operational knowledge and software stack for government-coordinated AI training at scale — not just for nuclear applications, but for the broader national security AI portfolio.

---

## Why the DOE Is Well-Positioned

The Department of Energy's combination of assets makes it unusually well-positioned for large-scale AI development. The DOE operates the most powerful scientific supercomputers in the world, maintained by world-class systems engineering teams. The national labs have decades of experience with large-scale scientific computing. And the DOE's mission — encompassing nuclear security, energy infrastructure, and basic science — creates natural use cases for AI capabilities that overlap significantly with frontier AI development.

Critically, the DOE has institutional experience with the security and classification issues that make government AI development different from corporate AI development. Training models on classified data, operating in air-gapped environments, and managing the export control implications of advanced AI are all problems the national labs have been grappling with for years. That experience is a meaningful competitive advantage as AI development increasingly intersects with national security considerations.

---

## Implications for the National AI Strategy

These two projects suggest that the US government is developing something it hasn't had before: a credible option for government-coordinated frontier AI development that doesn't depend on the major commercial AI labs. The current US AI strategy relies heavily on private sector leadership — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta. Government compute is important but has mostly played a supporting role, providing research funding and access to specialized infrastructure.

If the DOE's tri-lab training approach scales up — and if the NNSA-style pooled compute model proves viable — the government could become a more direct participant in frontier AI development, not just a customer or facilitator. This would have significant implications for the competitive dynamics between US and Chinese AI development, for the governance of dual-use AI capabilities, and for the relationship between government and industry in shaping AI trajectories.

---

## Risks and Open Questions

There are legitimate concerns about this trajectory. Government AI development creates risks around democratic accountability, transparency, and the potential for AI capabilities to be developed in ways that serve narrow institutional interests rather than broad public benefit. The national labs operate with significant secrecy around their work, and AI systems developed in those environments may have properties — safety-relevant or otherwise — that are not subject to external review.

At the same time, the alternative — a future where frontier AI development is entirely dominated by a small number of private corporations accountable primarily to shareholders — has its own significant risks. The question of what governance structures can balance the competing demands of safety, security, innovation, and accountability is one that the AI community and the policy community will need to work through together.
