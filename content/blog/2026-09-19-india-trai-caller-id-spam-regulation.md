# India Mandates Caller-ID Apps to Share Spam Data with Telecom Operators

India's telecom regulator has issued a new directive requiring call-ID and call-management applications to share their spam reporting data with telecom operators -- a move that Truecaller, the Scandinavian company with over 500 million monthly active users and 350 million users in India alone, has criticized as "anti-competitive" and a threat to user privacy.

---

## TRAI's New Rules: Broadening the Spam Enforcement Pool

On Friday, the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) amended rules governing commercial communications, making it mandatory for call-ID and call-management apps that let users flag calls as spam or junk to transmit those reports to a blockchain-based platform maintained by telecom operators.

TRAI stated the change is intended to broaden the pool of spam reports available for action against spammers, connecting reports collected by apps with the telecom industry's enforcement infrastructure. The regulator also clarified that calls made automatically -- including those using AI-generated or AI-assisted voices -- fall under its application-to-person (A2P) framework, requiring companies to declare such systems to their telecom operators in advance.

---

## Truecaller's Largest Market Under Threat

India represents Truecaller's largest market globally, accounting for over 350 million of its more than 500 million monthly active users. The company uses community reports alongside automated detection to identify and block spam calls, and estimates its Indian users encountered approximately 42 billion spam calls in 2025.

Truecaller previously objected to restrictions preventing call-management apps from automatically labeling calls from government-designated number ranges as spam. Friday's amendments retain those restrictions, prohibiting blanket blocking, filtering, or spam-tagging of calls from designated number series used for promotional, service, and transactional communications.

The company called the new requirement a "one-way exchange" that hands commercially valuable proprietary data to telecom operators -- without equivalent data flowing back to app providers.

---

## Privacy and Enforcement Questions

Policy experts have raised concerns about what information apps will actually be required to transmit, how users will be notified or asked for consent, and how that data can subsequently be retained and used. A March draft proposed using India's IT laws to enforce the requirement, but it remains unclear whether that mechanism was retained in the final rules.

The amendments also introduce a termination charge of up to 5 paise (approximately 0.052 US cents) per minute for A2P calls, with exemptions for certain designated number ranges.

---

## Technical and Jurisdictional Complexities

Sumeysh Srivastava, a partner at New Delhi-based consulting firm The Quantum Hub, noted that the new rules bridge two distinct layers: telecom operators provide the underlying network and run the blockchain-based anti-spam system, while caller-ID apps operate closer to end users. This division raises technical and jurisdictional questions about reporting standards and enforcement.

The key test, Srivastava said, is how a call is initiated rather than simply whether it uses an AI-generated voice -- leaving uncertainty around AI-assisted calls that involve human initiation.
