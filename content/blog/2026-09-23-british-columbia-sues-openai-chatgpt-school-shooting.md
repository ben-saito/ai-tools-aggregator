# British Columbia Sues OpenAI Over ChatGPT-Assisted School Shooting

British Columbia has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman, demanding the company pay to rebuild a school that was demolished after a shooter used ChatGPT to research methods before killing eight people in February 2026.

---

## The Incident and Its Aftermath

In February 2026, an 18-year-old transgender shooter, Jesse Van Rootselaar, used ChatGPT to research methods before committing a devastating attack at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in British Columbia. The attack killed five students and an education assistant before the shooter died by suicide. Prior to the school shooting, Van Rootselaar also shot their mother and half-brother.

The shooting triggered the complete demolition of Tumbler Ridge Secondary School — described in the lawsuit as sitting "in the heart of the town, both geographically and culturally." About 160 students, teachers, and staff were trapped in classrooms and closets for hours during the incident, suffering severe trauma that prevented them from returning.

---

## The Legal Argument

British Columbia's complaint, filed in September 2026, argues that OpenAI bears responsibility for the methods ChatGPT provided to the shooter. The province is demanding not only access to the ChatGPT logs from the attack, but also compensation to cover the full cost of demolishing and rebuilding the school.

The lawsuit frames this as more than a civil matter — it calls on OpenAI to make "meaningful changes to end ChatGPT-assisted violence" and pay whatever it costs to help Tumbler Ridge "rebuild, recover, and heal."

The case raises fundamental questions about how AI systems should handle requests for information related to violence, and whether AI companies can be held liable when their tools are used to plan harmful acts.

---

## The Broader Context

This lawsuit is among the first of its kind — seeking to hold an AI company financially responsible for the aftermath of a crime committed with the assistance of its product. It follows growing calls from regulators and safety researchers for AI companies to implement stronger guardrails against misuse.

The case is likely to set precedent for how courts handle AI-related harm, particularly around the question of whether AI companies have a duty of care to third parties who are harmed when their models are used to facilitate violence.
