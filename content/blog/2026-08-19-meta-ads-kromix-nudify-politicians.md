# Meta Ran Ads for an App Promising to Nudify Female Politicians

*Ars Technica, August 19, 2026*

Meta's advertising platform ran promotional videos for an AI image tool called Kromix that explicitly promised to generate deepfake nudes of female politicians, according to a Wired report published August 19. The ads were viewed by Ars Technica and others, and featured a voice-over declaring the tool had "no restrictions" and depicted real women including a prominent female US politician.

---

## What the Ads Showed

Kromix describes itself as an "AI image styler." One video ad featured a woman closely resembling a prominent female US politician in front of a US flag, with the caption "What if she moved?" followed by a wink — before the ad cut to additional content promoting the tool's unrestricted capabilities.

The voice-over in the advertisements promoted the app as having "no restrictions" and described it as "the AI that men actually use." WIRED reviewed multiple ads for the platform.

Meta told Wired it is investigating the ads and that they violate the company's policies against deepfake content. The ads were served despite Meta's stated restrictions on AI-generated content depicting real people in explicit contexts.

---

## The Broader Context: Deepfake Ads on Major Platforms

This incident is not isolated. The discovery highlights ongoing challenges for major advertising platforms in moderating AI-generated content at scale. While Meta and other platforms have published policies against deepfake porn and non-consensual intimate imagery, enforcement at the ad-unit level remains inconsistent.

Kromix appears to have specifically targeted political figures — a particularly sensitive category given the role of visual media in political perception. Political deepfakes have been identified by election security experts as a significant threat to democratic processes, with the 2026 US midterms approaching.

---

## Meta's Policy Response

Meta's community standards prohibit "sexualized representations of real people" and content created with AI to circumvent this. The company stated it removed the ads upon being notified and is reviewing its ad review processes to prevent similar content from reaching its platform again.

The incident raises questions about whether automated ad review systems are sufficiently calibrated to detect deepfake promotional content, particularly when it is framed as an "image styling" tool rather than explicitly described as a nudify application.

---

## Why This Matters for AI Safety

The Kromix case illustrates a gap between AI safety research and consumer-facing AI products. While leading AI labs and safety organizations have published principles against generating non-consensual intimate imagery, the technical barrier to creating such content has decreased substantially. Open-weight image models combined with targeted prompts can produce results that are difficult for automated systems to distinguish from legitimate content.

Regulatory frameworks in the US have yet to establish federal standards for AI-generated intimate imagery, though several states have passed laws specifically addressing deepfake porn. The Federal Election Commission has begun examining whether AI-generated content in political advertising requires additional disclosure.
