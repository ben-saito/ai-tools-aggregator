# AI Systems Are Excellent Engineers But Poor Researchers: New Study via Shadow Evaluation

New research conducted by a broad academic coalition — including Princeton, UC Berkeley, Georgetown CSET, Johns Hopkins, Stanford, and the UK AI Security Institute — has produced a striking result: today's frontier AI systems can solve complex engineering problems but cannot generate novel research ideas that advance the field of AI. The work, called "shadow evaluation," provides the most direct evidence yet that current AI systems lack the kind of tasteful creativity required for recursive self-improvement.

---

## What Is Shadow Evaluation?

The research team partnered with the authors of two papers submitted to NeurIPS 2026 that were not yet public. They then gave frontier AI agents — specifically Claude Opus 4.8 running within the OpenClaw agentic framework — the central research questions from those unpublished papers and asked the agents to produce complete research contributions.

The original paper authors then evaluated the AI-generated research as they would a conference submission, unaware it came from an AI system. The results were striking in their consistency of failure: both AI-generated papers were rejected, one scored a 2 ("Reject") and the other a 1 ("Strong Reject"). Human reviewers highlighted the same deficiencies in both: poorly motivated data and experiments, no novel contribution, and impenetrable prose.

---

## Engineering vs. Research: A Crucial Distinction

The research team found a clear divide in AI capabilities. AI systems excel at engineering problems — decomposing a known goal into technical steps, writing code, running experiments, and iterating on implementations. These are the core capabilities that make AI coding assistants valuable today.

But research at the frontier of human knowledge requires something different: the ability to identify promising research directions, recognize when an approach is unlikely to work, find novel angles on problems, and know when to abandon a promising-seeming line of inquiry in favor of something better. The AI systems in this study failed consistently at these metacognitive tasks.

Specifically, the AI systems committed to narrow research paths very early and failed to respond to feedback about how to improve their experimental designs. They found it nearly impossible to reverse out of unpromising approaches and explore alternatives. This rigidity — useful for engineering tasks where the goal is clear — is devastating for research where the goal itself may be wrong.

---

## Why This Matters for the Intelligence Explosion Debate

One of the central questions in AI safety research is how quickly AI systems might be able to automate AI research itself — a recursive self-improvement scenario sometimes called the "intelligence explosion" or "slow takeoff" debate. If AI systems can reliably improve themselves, the rate of capability gain could accelerate dramatically.

This study provides a data point suggesting that today's systems cannot yet do creative research at a publishable level. The shadow evaluation result implies that there is still a meaningful human role in directing research — identifying which problems are worth solving and which approaches are promising. These are not engineering challenges but something closer to scientific taste or intuition.

However, the researchers are careful to note that this is early evidence from a limited number of cases. The study used two research projects; whether the results generalize broadly is an open question. And given how rapidly AI capabilities are improving, the window during which humans maintain this advantage may be narrowing.
