# Anthropic Details Alleged "Distillation" Attacks by China AI Companies — Alibaba, Moonshot, DeepSeek

Anthropic published a report on September 11 accusing three China-based AI companies — Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek — of persistently using a technique called "distillation" to replicate Anthropic's models. Distillation involves using outputs from another company's model as inputs to train one's own model, which Anthropic claims violates its API terms of service and AI safety standards.

---

## Details of the "Persistent Distillation Attacks"

According to Anthropic's report, Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek have been repeatedly calling Anthropic's models through its API and using the outputs to train their own models. The report states that these attacks are persistent and have been observed over several months as competition in the AI space has intensified among China-based companies.

Anthropic stated in the report: "These attacks are persistent, and our teams have been monitoring them continuously over the past several months." The company claims this behavior constitutes a violation of API usage agreements and potentially amounts to theft of intellectual property.

Anthropic indicated it plans to implement specific countermeasures, including further API access restrictions and blocking of traffic suspected to be used for distillation purposes.

---

## Reactions from China AI Companies

Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek have not provided official comments on the report. All three companies have reportedly used various techniques, including distillation, to catch up to US AI companies in recent years.

Industry analysts note that the report is likely to become a new flashpoint in US-China AI competition. It is unusual for a leading AI lab like Anthropic to publicly name and criticize competitors in this manner, and the move is expected to send ripples through the industry.

---

## Legal and Ethical Questions

Distillation attacks differ from the traditionally accepted practice of "knowledge distillation," which involves training on publicly available information with fewer copyright concerns.

However, as Anthropic argues, repeatedly using API outputs to train competing models likely constitutes a breach of API terms of service. Most AI company policies explicitly prohibit using outputs for training competing AI systems.

This issue is further complicated by the intersection of AI safety and international competition. The question of whether distillation attacks provide an effective means for Chinese AI companies to catch up to US competitors is now being posed directly to the industry.
