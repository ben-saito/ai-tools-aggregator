# Trump China AI Race: Experts Warn "AI Race" Narrative Endangers US

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that the US and China had discussed setting up an AI safety notification mechanism ahead of a scheduled Trump-Xi meeting. But experts are warning that Trump's "AI race" framing may undermine the very cooperation needed to address genuine AI risks.

---

## A Proposal Without Technical Depth

The proposed alert system would connect government officials with no AI expertise, rather than researchers or engineers who could assess reported risks. Sources familiar with the plan described the technical qualifications of the proposed channel as "BS."

"The scientific, technical term is BS," one source told Ars Technica. The channel would reportedly include no officials with technical expertise who could be trusted to evaluate AI safety risks.

Sam Bresnick, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, noted that an existing communication channel is better than none, but the absence of technically qualified personnel undermines the mechanism's purpose.

---

## Export Controls Complicate Trust

The proposal faces additional headwinds from US-China tech rivalry. Trump has steadily increased export controls explicitly designed to block China from accessing technology to advance AI. Experts say China may not trust AI safety conversations that are framed around limiting China's capabilities.

China has yet to publicly acknowledge the proposed alert system. Even if China agreed to participate, people familiar with the plan told Politico that the monitoring mechanism appears superficial.

---

## The "AI Race" Narrative Under Scrutiny

Alvin Wang Graylin, a global technology strategist at Stanford Digital Economy Lab, told Ars that the US is too caught up in a "deeply flawed" narrative about "winning" an "AI race" that will likely benefit no one.

In reality, there are at least two races: one for intelligence and one for market dominance. Currently the US is making more money on frontier AI but is falling behind in the race for users globally. In the last six months, adoption of China's open models has exploded.

"US-China capability gaps at the frontiers of AI technology typically last only a few months, and there is no competitive 'moat' that will secure a permanent advantage to either nation," Graylin wrote in a September article for the Quincy Institute.

Graylin argued that the US pretending China achieving artificial superintelligence first would pose the "greatest AI security threat to the United States" is flawed, and is obstructing cooperation that would help both countries prepare for actual imminent AI risks.

"The most dangerous models are often those smaller specialized ones that could be created or utilized by non-state rogue actors for the purpose of deploying biological, chemical, or cyber weapons," Graylin wrote.

---

## What Experts Say Should Happen

Bresnick suggested that ongoing talks could be an opportunity for both sides to explore how each views AI risks. While the US has been combating public fears of uncontrollable AI, in China "there's just not the same fear right now about AI," largely because the state feels it can control AI.

Representative Ro Khanna suggested that talks between Trump and Xi should go further than setting up a communication line between officials. He argued for an international agreement banning recursive self-improving AI and safeguards preventing AI misuse for biological or nuclear weapons.

Graylin proposed concrete steps: sharing high-level intelligence, opening bilateral and multilateral channels on AI safety, loosening export controls that are likely preventing China from conducting better safety testing, and following China's lead by enacting reasonable domestic safety regulations.

"We cannot manage the multifaceted risks of the most powerful technology in human history while refusing to talk to the other nation building it," Graylin wrote.
