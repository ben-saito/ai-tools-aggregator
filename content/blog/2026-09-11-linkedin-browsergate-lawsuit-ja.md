# LinkedIn、BrowserGate訴訟を撃退 -- 裁判所がプライバシー侵害主張を弃却

LinkedIn won its motion to dismiss in the "BrowserGate" class action lawsuits on September 9 (US time), with US District Judge Vince Chhabria ruling that the plaintiffs failed to allege a viable privacy violation.

## Judge Chhabria's Ruling

Judge Chhabria granted LinkedIn's motion to dismiss while giving plaintiffs leave to amend their complaints. In his ruling, he expressed deep skepticism: "Given LinkedIn's further arguments that users voluntarily download browser extensions, which by their nature intentionally expose data to websites, it seems unlikely that the plaintiffs will ever be able to allege a privacy violation, much less prevail at the end of the day."

The case centered on allegations that LinkedIn was illegally scanning users' computers to identify Chrome browser extensions they had installed. The plaintiffs -- California residents Nicholas Farrell and Jeff Ganan -- filed separate class action lawsuits in April seeking to represent all LinkedIn users.

## Plaintiffs May Appeal or Refile in State Court

Ganan's attorney, J.R. Howell, told Ars Technica the firm is evaluating whether to bring the claims in California state court -- which has different requirements for standing -- or to appeal to the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. "The federal court determined that it lacked jurisdiction to hear the LinkedIn users' claims," Howell said. "The court did not adjudicate whether LinkedIn's surveillance practices were lawful. The ruling is not a vindication of the mass surveillance program alleged in our complaint."

The dismissal marks a significant procedural victory for LinkedIn, though the underlying privacy questions about browser extension scanning remain contested. The company's privacy policy does disclose that it uses cookies and similar technologies to collect information about users' web browsers and other devices -- a point LinkedIn cited as sufficient notice to users.

## Browser Extension Scanning: New Legal Frontier

The BrowserGate cases represent one of the first major legal tests of whether companies can scan users' browsers for extension data without violating privacy laws. The outcome could set precedent for how companies leverage browser extension information for business purposes. Judge Chhabria's skepticism toward the plaintiffs' theories suggests courts may be reluctant to expand privacy liability theories to cover browser scanning conducted with disclosed data practices.
