# Roundtables: A Conversation With the Creator of AI-Designed Viruses

---

In a wide-ranging roundtable discussion, Dr. Jennifer Okafor, a computational biologist at the Broad Institute, shared insights from her work using artificial intelligence to design novel viral sequences. Her research sits at the intersection of synthetic biology, AI safety, and biosecurity.

Okafor's laboratory has developed AI systems capable of generating viral genome sequences that could theoretically infect human cells. While the work is conducted under strict biosafety protocols and has undergone extensive ethical review, the research has attracted scrutiny from biosecurity experts who question whether such sequences should be designed at all.

"The goal is not to create pathogens," Okafor clarified. "The goal is to understand the design space of viral evolution well enough that we can develop better defenses." Her team uses generative models to explore how viral genomes might evolve under different selective pressures, generating hypotheses that guide experimental work.

The AI systems in question are not publicly available, reflecting concerns about potential misuse. Access is limited to verified researchers within Okafor's institution, and all generated sequences undergo review by a biosafety committee before any experimental work proceeds.

Critics argue that the research, regardless of intent, provides a roadmap for bad actors. "You cannot unlearn knowledge," noted one biosecurity researcher not involved in the work. "Once something is demonstrated to be possible, the information spreads."

Okafor acknowledged these concerns directly. "There is a genuine tension between open scientific inquiry and biosecurity. I don't have a clean answer." She pointed to her team's publication record, which omits specific sequence designs while still conveying scientific findings.

The discussion touched on how AI safety research could apply to biosecurity contexts. Ideas borrowed from AI alignment—such as interpretability tools that reveal what a model has "learned"—might eventually help identify potentially dangerous biological designs before they are synthesized.

The roundtable format allowed for substantial back-and-forth between Okafor and attendees with diverse backgrounds in science, policy, and ethics. No consensus emerged, but the conversation highlighted the growing need for frameworks governing dual-use biological research in the AI era.
