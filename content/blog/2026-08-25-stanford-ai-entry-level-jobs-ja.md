# Stanford Study: AI Is Hitting Entry-Level Jobs Hardest

---

## The Finding: Young Workers Bear the Brunt of AI Disruption

A new study from Stanford University has found that AI is disproportionately affecting entry-level jobs, with young workers in AI-impacted fields experiencing a 19% decline in employment compared to more AI-resistant occupations. The research adds quantitative weight to what labor economists have been observing anecdotally: the automation wave triggered by advances in generative AI is not hitting all segments of the workforce equally.

The study, which analyzed employment trends across multiple sectors, found that roles typically considered entry points into professional careers, such as junior analytical positions, basic data entry, and initial customer service functions, are the most exposed to AI-driven automation. These are the jobs that have historically served as pipelines for career development and economic mobility for younger workers entering the job market.

The findings suggest that the economic disruption from AI is not simply a story of machines replacing human labor wholesale. Instead, it is a more nuanced process where AI first colonizes the tasks that were traditionally the domain of newcomers to a profession, effectively cutting off the bottom rungs of career ladders in affected industries.

---

## The Mechanism: What Makes Entry-Level Work Vulnerable

The structural reason for entry-level vulnerability is not difficult to understand. Entry-level positions are characterized by relatively standardized, rule-based tasks that lend themselves well to automation. A junior analyst might spend much of their early career compiling data, running routine analyses, and producing standardized reports. A customer service representative at entry level handles common, frequently occurring queries following established scripts. These are precisely the tasks that modern AI systems, particularly large language models, excel at.

More senior roles, by contrast, tend to involve greater complexity, judgment calls, stakeholder management, and unstructured problem-solving. While AI is making inroads into these areas as well, the rate of displacement is slower because the work requires contextual understanding and decision-making that current AI systems handle less reliably.

The implication is that industries most exposed to AI disruption may face a structural hollowing out of their workforce pyramid. If AI handles the entry-level work that traditionally trains and develops future senior professionals, the pipeline for developing experienced talent could be disrupted in ways that create long-term skill gaps.

---

## Labor Market Implications and Policy Responses

The Stanford findings have significant implications for both labor market policy and education policy. If entry-level jobs continue to erode, the traditional pathway through which young workers gain experience and advance into higher-skill, higher-pay roles will be disrupted for entire sectors of the economy.

Some labor economists have proposed that the solution lies in restructuring how professional development works, with more emphasis on simulation-based training and apprenticeship models that do not depend on entry-level employment as the primary mechanism for skill transfer. Others argue that the burden should fall on educational institutions to better prepare graduates for roles that are complementary to, rather than substitutable by, AI systems.

The 19% employment decline figure for young workers in AI-exposed fields is likely to become a reference point in policy debates about how to manage the transition. It represents one of the more concrete quantitative estimates of AI's labor market impact to date, grounded in empirical analysis rather than projection models.

The study adds to a growing body of research suggesting that the AI transition will require deliberate policy interventions to manage its distributional effects. Without such interventions, the benefits of AI-driven productivity gains may accrue disproportionately to capital owners and highly skilled workers, while younger and less experienced workers bear a disproportionate share of the adjustment costs.
