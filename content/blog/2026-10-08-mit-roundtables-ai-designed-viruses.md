# MIT Roundtables: Inside the Conversation About AI-Designed Viruses

---

MIT Technology Review's Roundtables series sat down with researchers working at the intersection of AI and synthetic biology to discuss a question that has moved from science fiction to policy agendas: what happens when AI systems can design novel viruses, and how should we prepare for that future?

## The Conversation

The roundtable brought together a subset of MIT's Innovators Under 35 honorees — researchers working on AI tools for protein engineering, drug discovery, and biosecurity. The discussion ranged from technical capabilities to ethical frameworks to regulatory gaps.

A recurring theme was dual-use risk: the same AI systems that can design therapeutic proteins to cure diseases can, in principle, be directed toward designing pathogens. Unlike traditional biological weapons development, which required significant infrastructure and expertise, AI-designed biology lowers the barrier to entry substantially.

## The Current State of AI in Biology

Several participants noted that current AI systems for protein design are impressive but not yet at the level of being able to design functional viruses from scratch. The more immediate concern is using AI to enhance existing pathogens — making them more transmissible, more lethal, or more resistant to vaccines.

Protein structure prediction models like AlphaFold have already transformed drug discovery by accurately predicting how proteins fold. The next generation of models aims to go beyond prediction to design entirely new proteins with desired properties — a capability that accelerates both beneficial and harmful applications.

## Biosecurity in the Age of AI

The conversation turned to what biosecurity looks like when the threat model includes AI-designed biology. Traditional biosecurity focuses on known pathogens and state-level programs. AI introduces a new vector: individual actors with laptop-level resources and access to model outputs.

Proposed responses include:
- **Compute thresholds**: Requiring justification for large-scale protein design training runs
- **Output filtering**: Technical approaches to prevent models from generating dangerous sequences
- **International norms**: Agreements similar to chemical weapons conventions but for AI-generated biology

## What's Missing

Participants generally agreed that current governance frameworks are inadequate. The speed of AI development outpaces regulatory cycles, and international coordination remains difficult. The US government has issued some guidance on synthetic biology security, but enforcement mechanisms are limited.

The technical community's role in self-governance was debated. Some researchers argue for pre-publication review of potentially dangerous findings — similar to dual-use research of concern (DURC) frameworks in biology. Others worry this would chill beneficial research without meaningfully reducing biosecurity risk.

## Looking Forward

The roundtable did not produce a consensus solution, but it clarified the contours of the problem. AI-designed viruses remain a future concern rather than a present crisis, but the gap between now and that future is closing. The question is whether governance frameworks can close it faster.
