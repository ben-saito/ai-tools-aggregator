# Review: 4-Hour Musk Documentary Feels Like Two Different Films

---

Alex Gibney's provocative film is compelling but overlong, and there's not much new here for regular Ars readers.

---

Elon Musk is a very familiar figure to Ars readers, given his massive influence on the technology sector over the last 20 years. Oscar-winning director Alex Gibney's latest biographical documentary — simply titled *Musk* — covers a lot of well-known ground and challenges the carefully crafted public image Musk has cultivated as a visionary tech titan.

Gibney's documentary presents Musk as more of a Gilded Age confidence man, rejiggered for the 21st century: an investor cosplaying as an inventor whose real genius is pumping stocks and dazzling investors with visions of a high-tech utopian future. The film is bound to push all the right outrage buttons, whether one is a Musk fan or a detractor.

Gibney has been making documentaries since the 1980s, covering subjects from Jimi Hendrix and Hunter S. Thompson to WikiLeaks, Scientology, Steve Jobs, Elizabeth Holmes, and the opioid epidemic. He won an Oscar for Best Documentary Feature for *Taxi to the Dark Side* in 2007.

The documentary traces Musk's journey from South Africa through PayPal, SpaceX, Tesla, and his more recent ventures. Several former associates appear on camera to share their perspectives, painting a portrait of a brilliant but difficult leader. The film examines both the technological ambition and the personal cost of Musk's leadership style.

However, the documentary suffers from an identity crisis — it can't quite decide whether it's a critical biography or hagiography. The first half focuses heavily on the entrepreneurial drama, while the second half veers into territory that feels more sympathetic to its subject. The result raises important questions about the intersection of genius and ego in tech leadership.

For Ars readers who've tracked Musk's various ventures and controversies over the years, the documentary retreads much familiar territory. The most compelling sections explore the human cost of Musk's management on his employees.

Jennifer Ouellette for Ars Technica, October 11, 2026.
