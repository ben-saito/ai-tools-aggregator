# The Ecologist Using AI to Reveal Invisible Networks That Revive Rainforests

---

Massive tree-planting programs often lead to unhealthy monocrop plantations that fail to restore the biodiversity and ecosystem services of native forests. Daisy Dent, a senior scientist at ETH Zurich, has spent fifteen years documenting the underground networks that connect rainforest trees — fungal threads that shuttle nutrients, water, and chemical signals between plants sometimes hundreds of meters apart. Her work combines traditional field ecology with machine learning analysis of soil samples to map and understand these hidden biological networks.

Tropical forests like these are our planet's biggest terrestrial carbon sink, accounting for around 40% of carbon stored on land. Dent's research aims to establish low-cost, high-impact alternatives to typical carbon-crediting schemes, which often plant huge areas with a single species of tree and fail to restore the complex ecological relationships that make forests resilient.

---

## Mycorrhizal Networks: Nature's Internet

The networks, called mycorrhizal fungi, are not passive conduits for nutrient transfer. Dent's research shows they actively redistribute carbon and nitrogen based on signals from stressed trees — a kind of emergency relief system encoded in the forest's biology.

Forests that retain these networks recover from clear-cutting years faster than those without fungal connectivity. A single mature tree can be connected to dozens of neighbors via mycorrhizal threads, creating an underground communication and resource-sharing infrastructure that has fascinated ecologists for decades.

---

## Machine Learning as an Ecological Tool

What sets Dent's work apart is the integration of machine learning to scale her observations across vast forest areas. Soil samples contain millions of fungal spores and root fragments — analyzing these by hand would take lifetimes. AI-powered image analysis and DNA sequencing classification allow her team to process samples at unprecedented scale.

Her models can predict forest recovery potential based on the density and diversity of mycorrhizal networks in a given area — information that could help conservation groups prioritize which degraded lands to protect rather than abandon.

---

## Implications for Climate Policy

The findings challenge prevailing carbon offset models that treat tree planting as a simple carbon sequestration equation. A monocrop plantation of fast-growing species may sequester carbon quickly, but it lacks the biodiversity and network complexity that make forests resilient over decades.

Natural forest regeneration — facilitated by intact mycorrhizal networks — can recreate ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide in just a few decades, often outperforming artificial plantations in long-term carbon storage and biodiversity metrics.
