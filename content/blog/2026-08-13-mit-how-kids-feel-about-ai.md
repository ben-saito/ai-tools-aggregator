# What Kids Actually Think About AI: MIT Technology Review Surveys the Next Generation

MIT Technology Review published a report this week documenting how children and teenagers feel about artificial intelligence — and the findings challenge several assumptions held by adults building AI products for education and consumer markets.

---

## The Survey

Researchers spoke with elementary and middle school students about their experiences and attitudes toward AI tools. Rather than finding a generation of enthusiastic early adopters, the report describes a cohort that is pragmatic, sometimes indifferent, and frequently ambivalent about AI's role in their lives.

Many of the students who could articulate detailed interests in music, sports, or gaming responded to questions about AI with words like "bruh" and "meh" — suggesting that AI has not yet become a topic of intrinsic interest for large portions of youth.

---

## Usage Patterns

The MIT findings align with Pew Research Center data from February 2026 showing that 57% of US teenagers have used a chatbot. But usage rates and attitudes toward AI as a subject are distinct: high usage does not translate to high engagement or strong opinions about the technology.

A notable subset of students reported positive experiences using AI for homework help and creative projects. However, a separate concern surfaces in the research: students who rely on chatbots instead of developing their own judgment or who trust AI-generated answers without verification may be building problematic epistemic habits.

---

## Adults May Be More Worried Than Kids

One striking finding is that adults — educators, policymakers, and parents — appear more anxious about AI's effects on children than the children themselves. The report suggests this adult-driven concern may reflect adult assumptions about what kids should be worried about, rather than children's own expressed priorities.

This disconnect has implications for how AI safety and literacy programs are designed. If interventions are calibrated to adult concerns rather than observed child behavior, they may miss the actual friction points in young people's AI use.

---

## What This Means for AI Developers

For companies building AI products for education or consumer markets, the report is a reminder that the target user population is not a monolithic bloc of "digital natives" enthusiastic about every new technology. Young users evaluate AI tools on practical utility, not on technical sophistication — and many find current AI products neither compelling nor concerning enough to form strong opinions about.

The kids are not asking for AI. Developers building for this segment may need to earn attention rather than assume it.
