# We can't help treating AI like it's human — but should we?

---

At MIT's Computer Science & Artificial Intelligence Lab (CSAIL), a pair of humanoid Unitree robots — each costing about $50,000 — were demonstrated for a tour group. The researcher guiding the tour noted that the robots "will tell you they cost $1 million" if asked, a reflection of how even AI researchers have begun attributing human-like personas to machines.

This anecdote illustrates a broader phenomenon: people instinctively treat AI systems as if they were human, attributing intentions, emotions, and social reasoning to algorithmic outputs. Researchers at MIT and elsewhere are studying the implications of this tendency as AI systems become more sophisticated.

The TechCrunch feature explores how the design of modern AI systems — particularly conversational agents and humanoid robots — deliberately exploits human psychological predispositions toward social cognition. Voice interfaces, named AI personas, and embodied robots all trigger social responses in users that differ fundamentally from how people interact with traditional software.

The article draws on research into human-robot interaction and the psychology of anthropomorphism, examining both the benefits (increased user engagement, trust) and risks (misplaced trust, dependency, manipulation) of designing AI systems that feel human.

The central question raised is whether the AI industry's push toward human-like AI is a deliberate design choice with safety implications, and whether the field should be more intentional about how AI systems are framed and presented to users.

As AI systems become more capable of sustained, context-aware conversation, the boundary between tool and social actor continues to blur — with consequences that researchers are only beginning to understand.
