# Trump Memo Authorizes Private Security Firms to Hack Overseas Cybercriminals

The Trump administration has issued a National Security Presidential Memorandum authorizing private sector companies to conduct offensive cyber operations against foreign transnational criminal organizations. The policy marks the first time the US government has officially permitted civilian firms to perform cyberattacks overseas.

---

## A New Era in Cybersecurity Policy

The National Security Presidential Memorandum, issued Thursday, directs the National Coordination Center (NCC) — operating under the Homeland Security Task Force — to develop a program for conducting cyber operations targeting foreign transnational criminal organizations (TCOs). The Departments of Justice and Homeland Security will provide oversight, with private sector companies as the primary execution arm.

The memorandum lists ransomware attacks, sextortion schemes, phishing campaigns, financial fraud, and impersonation scams as eligible targets. Participating firms may conduct "Cyber Surveillance Operations and Cyber Effects Operations" against cyber-enabled TCOs defined as foreign groups that "conduct cyber-enabled crime against the United States Government, a United States person, or United States interests."

## Guardrails and Escrow Requirements

The policy imposes specific constraints. Participating companies must be vetted and approved by the Departments of Justice and Homeland Security. Operations may not result in "Critical Outcomes" — meaning loss of life, serious injury, or actions "rise to the level of use of force or armed attack under international law."

Companies must deposit $1 million in an escrow account, forfeited upon non-compliance with contractual agreements. The Departments of Justice and Homeland Security have 60 days to define program specifics including minimum standards for participating companies: technical proficiency, proven performance, facility security, personnel vetting, and reliability.

## Industry Reception

Independent security researcher Kevin Beamont commented: "There's definitely merit in the idea of hacking ransomware groups and it does already in fact happen. But the correct incentives have gotta be there."

Beamont added: "The biggest problem I've had with fighting ransomware over the past 5 years is private cyber companies basically lobbying for nothing to change. A lot of companies have made a lot of money, so putting them in charge of stopping it seems optimistic."

The memorandum represents a significant shift from the previous prohibition on private sector offensive cyber operations, which previously required court-authorized approval for such actions.
