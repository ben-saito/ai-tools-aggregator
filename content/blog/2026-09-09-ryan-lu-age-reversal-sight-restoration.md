# MIT Tech Review: Geneticist's Age-Reversal Tech Enters Human Trials for Sight Restoration

Yuancheng (Ryan) Lu, a 34-year-old researcher at the Whitehead Institute, has developed a gene therapy that repaired damaged optic nerves in mice and has now advanced to human clinical trials for glaucoma patients. The therapy, now called ER-100, is the culmination of research begun during Lu's PhD at Harvard Medical School in 2018.

---

## From Mice to Humans: A Six-Year Journey

Lu first demonstrated the technique by crushing the optic nerves of mice, blinding them, then injecting a gene therapy designed to restore the cells to a youthful state. Sixteen days later, the nerves were growing back. The mice could see again, confirmed by tests using rotating bars of light. David Sinclair, the longevity scientist who led the lab, recalls Lu's text with a photo: "I see the future," Sinclair replied.

The approach centers on cellular reprogramming — the same age-restoring process that occurs naturally in embryos, which is why babies are born young. In 2006, Japanese researchers showed four genes known as OSKM could trigger this process in lab cells. Lu refined the cocktail to just OSK — removing the Myc gene that posed cancer risks — and demonstrated it on the accessible optic nerve system.

---

## ER-100 Enters Clinical Trials

On June 9, 2026, Life Biosciences injected ER-100 into the eye of a patient with glaucoma. The announcement triggered headlines calling it potentially paradigm-shifting for regenerative medicine. Lu notes the human therapy is "barely even changed" from what he built as a student.

The eye's accessibility makes it an ideal testing ground for reprogramming therapies. Lu has since identified additional genes responsible for protecting the retina from free radical damage — a primary cause of age-related macular degeneration.

---

## The Broader Anti-Aging Investment Landscape

Lu's work helped ignite an investment rush in reprogramming and anti-aging medicine. Altos Labs and NewLimit have attracted major funding from US tech billionaires. Lu has also engaged with Chinese businessman Zhong Shanshan, one of China's wealthiest individuals.

Despite the hype, Lu maintains a measured perspective. He disagrees with projections of human lifespans reaching 200 years, citing the complexity of aging across multiple systems. His focus is on targeted applications: "Six years ago, you couldn't talk about rejuvenation. We didn't use that word," he told MIT Technology Review. "But I think people have accepted the concept that you can really reverse molecular age."
