# Flock Tightens Police Surveillance Tech Rules Amid Backlash

Flock, the police-tech company operating one of the largest networks of license plate readers in the United States, announced significant changes to how officers can access its surveillance infrastructure. The decision comes as cities across the country are dropping contracts and courts are scrutinizing the constitutional boundaries of mass license plate surveillance.

---

## The Problem: Officers Abusing Surveillance Access

Flock's network consists of over 120,000 cameras mounted on vehicles and infrastructure nationwide, capturing license plate data at a scale that gives law enforcement unprecedented ability to track vehicle movement patterns. A recent Washington Post investigation identified 46 cases in which officers allegedly used Flock's system for unauthorized purposes — including stalking current or former romantic partners.

The core issue is that officers could search the nationwide database without meaningful oversight. A single query could reveal everywhere a specific vehicle had traveled over weeks or months, with no requirement to document a legitimate investigative purpose at the time of the search.

---

## New Safeguards: Case Numbers and Audit Logs

Flock's new policy requires officers to enter a criminal case number before conducting a database search. The system, launched as an optional feature last year, is now mandatory. The company is also expanding an automatic auditing system that analyzes search activity patterns to flag officers who may be conducting unauthorized searches.

The case-number requirement represents a baseline standard that civil liberties organizations have long advocated. However, the ACLU's research on similar requirements elsewhere found that officers frequently circumvent them by entering generic justifications like "investigation" or dismissive entries like "hehehe" — with one Oregon department entering exactly that string 20 times.

---

## The Loophole: No Independent Verification

Flock has not implemented any system to verify that the case numbers entered by officers correspond to genuine, open investigations. This means the safeguard can be bypassed without technical detection. The company is relying on its audit algorithms to catch abuse after the fact, rather than preventing it at the point of access.

Privacy advocates argue that meaningful reform requires either independent oversight of search requests or a warrant requirement for access to the historical location data that a nationwide camera network like Flock's can provide.

---

## Business Impact: Cities Dropping Contracts

The policy changes represent an attempt to retain municipal customers who have begun terminating their Flock contracts over surveillance concerns. Flock has faced pressure from both civil liberties groups and municipal governments concerned about the scope of mass license plate collection and the potential for abuse.

The new rules also position Flock to better defend itself in ongoing legal challenges regarding the constitutionality of warrantless license plate tracking. The company argues that its system serves legitimate law enforcement purposes, while critics maintain that the current implementation lacks sufficient safeguards against mission creep.
