# People-Search Tool Exposed Database of 9 Million Face Photos

---

## Security Researcher Discovers Unprotected Database at ClarityCheck

An independent security researcher has discovered that ClarityCheck, a people-search tool that allows users to look up individuals by uploading photos, left a database containing more than 9 million image files publicly accessible. The exposed dataset totaled approximately 450 GB and included what appeared to be profile photos and facial recognition data.

Researcher Jeremiah Fowler, who documented the finding, reported that the database was not protected by any authentication mechanism. Anyone who discovered the connection could have accessed the trove of facial images without restriction.

---

## How People-Finder Tools Work

ClarityCheck is one of several websites that have emerged in recent years offering reverse image search capabilities focused on personal identification. These services claim to search the web, public records, and other databases to help users identify people from photographs.

While ClarityCheck's terms of service require users to attest they have permission to upload photos of others, the practical reality is quite different. Individuals whose faces ended up in the database likely had no knowledge their images were being collected and stored by the service.

"If you're trying to find out who a person is, you might not have authorization or permission, so people might not know that their image had been dumped into this database that was public," Fowler told Ars Technica.

---

## Facial Recognition and Privacy Concerns

This incident highlights the growing ecosystem of companies that collect and monetize facial recognition data with minimal oversight. Unlike traditional background check services that operate under regulatory frameworks, many of these newer people-search tools operate in legal gray areas with limited accountability.

For individuals concerned about their digital privacy, the discovery underscores how difficult it has become to control biometric data once it enters commercial systems. Facial images are increasingly valuable for identification purposes, and the proliferation of low-cost, high-accuracy recognition technology has made it trivial to build large databases that can be queried at scale.

---

## Lack of Regulation for People-Search Services

Unlike financial services or healthcare providers, people-search websites face minimal federal regulation in the United States. While some states have passed laws restricting specific uses of biometric data, enforcement remains inconsistent. The result is a market where companies can amass and sell access to facial recognition data with limited legal consequences if that data is mishandled.

The ClarityCheck exposure is not an isolated incident. Similar discoveries of unprotected databases at people-finder services have occurred repeatedly over the past several years, suggesting that security practices in this industry lag well behind the sensitivity of the data being handled.
