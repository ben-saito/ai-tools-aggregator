# ChatGPT-using lawyer barred, fined $5,000 for submitting fake witness testimony in murder appeal

A New Mexico criminal defense attorney with more than 40 years of experience has been barred from appearing before the state's highest court and fined $5,000 after using ChatGPT to draft a brief containing entirely fabricated witness testimony in a murder case appeal.

---

## The case: murder conviction appeal gone wrong

Attorney John Aarons was hired by the family of a defendant to appeal a 2025 murder conviction. Oscar Renee Sandoval was sentenced to life in prison in February 2025 after being convicted of killing Shiereen Al-Jibury, who was his partner and the mother of his children.

In August 2025, Aarons submitted a brief to the New Mexico Supreme Court. Weeks later, the state filed a motion to strike portions of that brief after discovering its contents were fabricated.

The court order described a striking pattern of invented evidence:

- "Officer Michelle Amarillo" and "Officer Sanchez" -- entirely fabricated police witnesses
- "Manal Al-Jibury" and "Teresa Marquez" -- witnesses who never existed
- "Danny Stanton" and "Linda Stanton" -- fabricated witnesses who allegedly received threats
- "Mariah Chavez" -- another wholly invented person

The brief also misrepresented legal authority in citations to previous cases.

---

## "I didn't know that AI could hallucinate facts"

When questioned by the state Supreme Court on August 21, Aarons admitted he had fed a computer-generated transcript of the murder trial and related documents into ChatGPT. The AI system output the fake quotes and fabricated witness accounts that ended up in his legal filing.

"I believe I did confirm that the trial transcript was accurate," Aarons said, when asked if he verified the AI's output before submitting it.

"It's of little comfort to know that my stupidity is what brings us together this afternoon," Aarons told the court.

Aarons stated that the legal cases cited in his brief were not fake -- meaning they were real cases -- but that his descriptions of those cases were inaccurate. The court's response was unsympathetic: there is no material distinction between inaccurately describing a real case and citing a fake one, because the rules of candor to the court apply with equal force in either situation.

---

## Consequences and proceedings

The New Mexico Supreme Court:

- Barred Aarons from appearing before the court pending the outcome of any disciplinary board investigation
- Fined Aarons $5,000, payable to the State Bar of New Mexico Client Protection Fund
- Ordered the public defender office to appoint a new lawyer for the defendant
- Struck all previous briefs from the record
- The case will proceed in the court's 2026-27 term

Additional penalties may result from the separate disciplinary proceedings now underway.

---

## A cautionary tale for legal professionals

The case is among the most concrete illustrations to date of how generative AI systems can produce plausible-sounding but entirely fabricated information -- and how professionals who rely on those outputs without verification can face serious professional consequences.

Unlike previous cases where lawyers cited entirely fake legal precedents (the now-infamous "fake citations" phenomenon), this case involved invented factual testimony: names, quotes, and events that never occurred. This distinction matters in court, where factual accuracy is paramount and wherefabricated evidence can undermine the integrity of proceedings and harm clients.

Legal commentators have noted that the case underscores the need for rigorous human review of any AI-assisted legal work, particularly when AI outputs are used as primary sources rather than starting points for further investigation.
