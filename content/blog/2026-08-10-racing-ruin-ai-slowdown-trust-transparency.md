# Coordinated AI Slowdown Requires Trust and Transparency — MIT/Columbia Research

A new research paper from MIT and Columbia University analyzes why coordinated slowdowns between rival AI companies are difficult to achieve and what conditions might make them possible. The paper, titled "Racing to Ruin," applies game theory to the competition between frontier AI labs and examines whether firms developing powerful AI systems could agree to slow down development.

---

## The Core Problem: Coordination Without Trust

The researchers developed a model of R&D competition between two firms operating in the shadow of potential catastrophe. As firms scale AI capabilities, they raise the hazard of an event that permanently eliminates all flow payoffs — essentially, a disaster scenario where AI development becomes uncontrollable. The hazard comes from developing the technology itself, not from using it.

The key finding: achieving a stable slowdown requires two conditions. First, some level of **transparency** about technology development — firms must be able to observe whether rivals are actually slowing down. Second, firms must be able to **trust** that competitors are rational actors who will reciprocate once agreements are made.

---

## Sequential vs. Simultaneous Coordination

The paper identifies two types of coordination challenges. **Sequential coordination** requires one firm to stop first, gambling that the rival will reciprocate once news arrives. Here, faster news about competitor actions raises the prize of reciprocation — but also creates incentives to wait for confirmation rather than act first.

**Simultaneous coordination** requires firms to stop at the same time without being able to observe each other's actions. Faster news makes both stopping first and waiting to verify more attractive, which can paradoxically destroy early-stopping equilibria at intermediate trust levels.

---

## Transparency Is Double-Edged

The most counterintuitive result: transparency itself can undermine coordination. At intermediate trust levels, increasing transparency can first destroy the early-stopping equilibrium by making free-riding attractive — a firm might wait for confirmation that its rival stopped without stopping itself. Only at high transparency levels does the equilibrium get restored.

---

## Trust as the Decisive Factor

With low trust between firms, every equilibrium leads to racing — disaster arrives with probability one. With intermediate trust, both immediate stopping and racing are possible equilibria. With high trust, the probability that two rational firms race forever vanishes quadratically.

---

## Why This Matters for AI Governance

The researchers draw parallels to arms control regimes for nuclear weapons, where verification and trust-building measures enabled meaningful agreements. For AI, the implication is that any hope of coordinating slowdown requires regimes for sharing information about the state of AI development, plus tools for verifying that stated slowdown commitments are genuine.

The fewer options for managing AI R&D automation risks, the worse outcomes become — making research like this increasingly urgent as AI capabilities advance.
