# We Can't Help Treating AI Like It's Human. But Should We?

---

A growing body of psychological research confirms what many AI practitioners have suspected: humans spontaneously attribute human-like mental states — intentions, beliefs, emotions — to AI systems, even when those systems are explicitly described as lacking consciousness or interior experience.

Studies conducted across multiple labs show that users form implicit models of AI as a social actor, applying social norms and expectations to interactions with language models, recommendation systems, and autonomous agents. This occurs even among technically sophisticated users and despite explicit knowledge that the systems are not conscious.

The tendency creates both opportunities and risks. AI systems that are perceived as more human-like tend to receive more engagement and trust in certain contexts — a pattern harnessed effectively in customer service, education, and therapeutic applications. However, the same tendency can lead users to over-rely on AI outputs, defer inappropriately to AI judgment, or fail to maintain the critical distance necessary when systems produce incorrect or harmful outputs.

Researchers are exploring design interventions that might reduce unwarranted anthropomorphism without destroying the engagement benefits that make AI systems useful. These include explicit system self-descriptions that emphasize computational mechanisms over conversational affordances, and user interfaces that frame AI outputs as suggestions rather than assertions.

The fundamental question — whether building AI systems that feel human is a feature or a bug — remains unresolved, with competing perspectives holding that anthropomorphism is an inevitable and perhaps acceptable cost of human-AI interaction, versus a risk factor that should actively be designed against.
