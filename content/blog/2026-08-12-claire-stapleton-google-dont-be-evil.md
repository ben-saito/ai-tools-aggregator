# Former Googler Claire Stapleton's Book Details Company's Cultural Decline

Claire Stapleton, a former longtime Google employee who became a prominent internal critic, has published a memoir titled "Don't Be Evil" recounting her journey from company cheerleader to outspoken dissident, Ars Technica reported August 11, 2026.

---

## From Golden Ticket to disillusionment

Stapleton joined Google in 2007 straight out of college, describing the experience as "getting the golden ticket in Willy Wonka." Her early career at Google coincided with the company's meteoric rise, and she describes the period as deeply formative, offset only by an increasing awareness of the disparity between Google's public values and its internal practices.

Her first major disillusionment came during her tenure at Google Creative Lab in New York, which she describes as "half utopian, half dystopian and unable to tell the difference." Following her experience there, she wrote an essay for Elle magazine to process her complicated feelings toward her former employer.

---

## The Walkout and Retaliation

Stapleton became a central figure in Google's 2018 employee walkout, which protested the company's handling of sexual misconduct allegations. Six months later, she claims she was demoted during a restructuring as retaliation for her activism. She ultimately resigned and has now written her memoir to document how Google shifted from its founding ideals.

The book traces how Google's organizational structure changed significantly, with executives each receiving their own communications teams and the marketing department expanding substantially — changes she argues eroded the culture that once differentiated the company.

---

## A Larger Question About Tech Industry Culture

Stapleton describes her experience connecting with ex-Googlers at gatherings where employees shared similar stories. At some point, she says she began wondering if they had all been "in a cult" — a metaphor for how Google functioned as both identity and livelihood for its employees, making the eventual separation deeply psychological.

The book raises broader questions about how Silicon Valley companies transition from mission-driven startups to large public corporations, and what that shift costs in terms of the values that initially attracted talent.
