# macOS 27 Golden Gate: Apple Intelligence Required, Intel Support Dropped

macOS 27 Golden Gate represents the most significant shift in Apple's desktop operating system in years -- Apple Intelligence is no longer optional. Ars Technica's comprehensive review reveals that Golden Gate is both a "Snow Leopard update" and a major leap for Apple Intelligence, making AI features unavoidable in the new release.

Generative AI defines the Golden Gate release, from marketing to features to the chips it runs on. There used to be a toggle to turn off Apple Intelligence and delete the gigabytes of AI models it downloads, but that option is now gone. Users who want Apple Intelligence will find it everywhere; users who don't have no escape route.

---

## Apple Intelligence: AFM 3 Core and AFM 3 Core Advanced

Golden Gate introduces a split between older and newer Apple Silicon Macs. Apple Intelligence is powered by AFM 3 Core model, described as built "in collaboration with Google." For more demanding tasks, there's also AFM 3 Core Advanced, Apple's "most powerful on-device model," which requires additional hardware -- notably 12GB RAM, a requirement no current Apple Silicon Mac officially ships with.

This creates an unusual situation where the flagship AI feature requires hardware that doesn't yet exist in the Mac lineup.

---

## Intel Support Ended, M1 Becomes Minimum

Golden Gate is the first macOS release since the mid-2000s not to run on any Intel Macs. Six years after the beginning of the Apple Silicon era, Apple has dropped support for the last Intel Macs that could run macOS 26 Tahoe. The minimum requirement is now Apple M1 or better.

This means all Macs now support Apple Intelligence -- which never ran on Intel-based hardware.

---

## Beyond Apple Intelligence: The Snow Leopard Treatment

Beneath the AI fanfare, the operating system underneath receives the kind of update macOS users usually appreciate. Golden Gate addresses the most irritating design sins of macOS 26 Tahoe, adds incremental improvements, and promises under-the-hood optimizations for common tasks.

The result is a combination that is occasionally jarring: big, hard-to-ignore and impossible-to-disable AI changes wedded to an understated update that in other years might have been called a "Snow Leopard release."
