# Hannah Earley's Vaire Aims to Build Reversible Computing Chips That Recycle Energy

A Cambridge PhD dropout turned chip startup founder is betting that the future of computing lies in chips that don't waste energy as heat. Hannah Earley, 31, has founded Vaire to commercialize "reversible computing" -- a chip architecture where information is never erased during computation, allowing energy to be recovered rather than dissipated.

---

## The Problem with Conventional Chips

Throughout the history of the computer chip, engineers have treated waste heat as an inevitable cost of computation. When conventional chips perform calculations, they erase information they no longer need, dissipating energy as heat in the process. Earley compares the approach to racing a car and slamming the brakes every few seconds -- converting useful kinetic energy into waste heat that can't be recovered.

This isn't a minor inefficiency. Data centers globally consume hundreds of terawatt-hours annually, with cooling systems adding further overhead. As AI workloads scale, chip energy efficiency has become both an economic and environmental imperative.

## Reversible Computing: Theory Meets Hardware

The concept of reversible computing was first proposed over 50 years ago by physicists who noted that the fundamental laws of thermodynamics don't require information to be erased. A computation is reversible if the input can always be deduced from the output -- meaning no information is destroyed during processing, and in theory, no energy needs to be dissipated.

While the idea was proposed decades ago, it proved impractical to implement with existing transistors and circuits. Standard logic gates are inherently irreversible -- they discard information. Earley has completely rethought the hardware needed to make reversibility work at scale, designing new circuit architectures where every step of computation preserves the ability to reconstruct prior states.

## From Cambridge to Vaire

Earley's journey into chip design started early. She began programming around age nine, starting with high-level coding for the web before digging into other languages. She enrolled in a PhD program at the University of Cambridge under computational biologist Gos Micklem, initially studying materials such as DNA for computation before redirecting toward the physical limits of computation itself.

She built software that could transform ordinary programs into reversible ones. "Eventually I wouldn't have to design the circuits myself," she told MIT Technology Review. The fascination completely redirected her PhD work toward the connection between information, energy, and heat.

After completing her degree in 2021, Earley met Rodolfo Rosini, a technology entrepreneur and investor. The pair cofounded Vaire that same year and have raised more than $12 million in funding. The startup is applying Earley's research to build practical reversible computing chips.

## The Road Ahead

The next challenge is making these drastically different chips fit into existing manufacturing systems and device form factors. Earley believes the future lies not in further refining conventional architectures but in fundamentally rethinking the relationship between information processing and energy consumption.

Igor Markov, a researcher in electronic design automation and former professor at the University of Michigan, said of the work: "It's clear they have something interesting." The technical hurdles remain substantial, but the potential efficiency gains -- orders of magnitude compared to conventional chips -- make reversible computing a promising direction for post-AI computing infrastructure.
