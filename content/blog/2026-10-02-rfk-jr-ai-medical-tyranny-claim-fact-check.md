# RFK Jr. Says AI Will Free Americans From "Medical Tyranny." The Data Says Otherwise

Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Secretary of Health and Human Services, told a Trump administration event that AI would allow Americans to "check" expert consensus on vaccines, masks, and public health measures -- freeing people from what he called "the tyranny of experts." Ars Technica put that claim to the test.

---

## The Claim

Kennedy claimed the Trump administration is "making it easy for every American to use AI," which he said would free citizens from reliance on expert guidance. He suggested AI tools would confirm his views on vaccine inefficacy, mask uselessness, and other contested public health claims.

"We're never, ever again going to be able to be dominated by public officials who tell us trust the experts, because every American will be able to check their work," Kennedy said.

He then cited specific examples where he said AI would contradict established science on masking, social distancing, and vaccine effectiveness.

---

## What Actually Happened When Ars Technica Asked AI

Ars Technica tested Kennedy's claim directly by asking major AI systems the same questions Kennedy implied they would answer differently.

On mask effectiveness, Google's **Gemini** responded: "Yes, masks work to reduce the spread of respiratory infectious diseases, operating primarily as physical barriers."

**ChatGPT** answered: "Yes. Masks can reduce the spread of some infectious diseases, particularly respiratory infections transmitted through droplets and aerosols."

Both systems confirmed the scientific consensus that Kennedy claimed they would contradict.

---

## The AI Reality Check Problem

The episode illustrates a broader challenge: AI systems trained on vast corpora of human knowledge tend to reflect mainstream expert consensus, not contrarian interpretations. Hallucination -- where AI generates plausible-sounding false information -- is a real problem. But for well-established scientific questions with strong evidence bases, AI systems generally reproduce established findings rather than amplifying misinformation.

Kennedy, who has no medical, scientific, or public health background, has repeatedly promoted discredited claims about vaccine safety. The administration's claim that AI would enable citizens to "check" expert consensus in ways that validate those claims appears not to match how current AI systems actually behave.

---

## Policy Context

The episode comes as the Trump administration finalizes its AI action plan and considers how to regulate AI in healthcare contexts. The administration has promoted AI as a tool for democratic access to information, while critics argue that AI systems can also amplify misinformation at scale.
