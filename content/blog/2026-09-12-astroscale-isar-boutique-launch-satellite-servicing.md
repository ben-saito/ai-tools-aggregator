# Satellite Servicing Companies Bet on Boutique Launch Providers as Market Shifts

Astroscale, the Japanese satellite servicing company, has signed contracts with Isar Aerospace for two dedicated launches of its satellites aboard Isar's Spectrum rocket. The deals, announced ahead of Isar's first orbital attempt, highlight how specialized satellite missions increasingly require tailored launch solutions that larger rideshare services cannot provide.

---

## The Case for Dedicated Launch

Satellite servicing and debris mitigation require precise orbital trajectories that rideshare missions cannot accommodate. Unlike bulk cargo heading to common orbits, servicing spacecraft must reach specific targets, often in less-traveled paths.

"Launch, as you well know, is the big issue around the industry writ large," said Chris Blackerby, Chief Operating Officer at Astroscale, in an interview with Ars Technica. "Dedicated launch is pretty essential for us for most of our missions. We could do a rideshare, but it's much more complicated."

The Spectrum rocket from Isar Aerospace targets the 1-ton payload class, positioning it to serve satellite servicing companies like Astroscale that operate spacecraft in the 500 to 700 kilogram range. This segment has seen consolidation, with competitors like Relativity Space and Astra exiting or scaling back their small launch efforts.

---

## Government Backing Drives Isar's Order Book

Isar's launch backlog consists primarily of government missions from the European Space Agency, the European Union, Norway, and Germany. These government customers have strategic interests in supporting a European launch capability, making them willing to commit to an unproven rocket.

Astroscale's contracts stand out as a commercial vote of confidence. The company is betting on Isar's technology before the rocket has demonstrated orbital flight, a risk that established satellite operators rarely take.

Among the Astroscale missions assigned to Isar is the ELSA-M spacecraft, which will capture and deorbit a defunct satellite in the Eutelsat OneWeb constellation to demonstrate end-of-life servicing capabilities. The ADRAS-J2 mission will pursue a separate servicing demonstration, both critical to Astroscale's commercial strategy.

---

## Boutique Launch Fills a Market Gap

The broader launch market has seen significant contraction in small-to-medium payload services. Firefly Aerospace remains the only other proven provider in the 1-ton class, though its Alpha rocket has faced development challenges.

"The only other proven launch provider in that 1-ton range is Firefly Aerospace, but they've had some issues with their Alpha rocket," Blackerby noted. "There were a bunch that were out there that were in that area for a while, including Relativity, they were aiming for that, and Astra was in that."

Katalyst Space, a US-based competitor, took a different path, choosing the Pegasus XL air-launched rocket for its July servicing mission. Both approaches highlight the specialized nature of satellite servicing launch requirements.

The segment remains small but strategically important as operators increasingly recognize that servicing satellites in orbit requires the same precision as launching them.
