# AI Has Not Displaced New College Graduates, Research Data Shows

Two separate studies released in 2026 reached opposing conclusions on whether AI is replacing entry-level jobs. New Census data shows no statistically significant increase in unemployment among recent college graduates, while an ADP payroll study found AI exposure correlated with job losses. The discrepancy highlights the difficulty of measuring AI's labor market impact with currently available data.

---

## Census Data Shows Normal Unemployment for Recent Graduates

Researchers Robert Fairlie and Jane Wu analyzed Current Population Survey data from the US Census Bureau to study employment trends among recent college graduates aged 22 to 25. Their findings, published by CESifo, show that the summer 2026 unemployment rate for young college graduates was 7.3 percent, within the historical range of 6.3 percent in 2022 to 7.8 percent in 2024.

The study found no statistically significant difference in unemployment trends between recent college graduates and either non-college graduates in the same age range or older college graduates aged 30 to 49. When broken down by AI exposure as measured by occupation, the results showed no meaningful correlation between AI exposure and employment outcomes for recent graduates.

The researchers describe this as a useful early indicator. They note that if AI were causing widespread displacement among entry-level workers, the effects would likely appear first in hiring rates rather than in existing employment. The data so far shows hiring has not been notably affected.

---

## ADP Payroll Data Tells a Different Story

A separate study using ADP payroll data found that workers in jobs with higher AI exposure faced elevated risks of displacement. This study, conducted by Stanford researchers, used HR firm ADP's proprietary dataset covering a substantial cross-section of the economy.

The discrepancy between the two studies likely reflects differences in methodology and data sources. The Census data captures a broader population including part-time workers and those not covered by payroll records, while ADP data may miss gig workers, small business employees, and sectors underrepresented in the dataset.

The CESifo researchers acknowledge that ADP data can reveal patterns not visible in broader surveys, but caution that neither dataset provides a complete picture of AI's current labor market effects.

---

## Why the Impact on New Grads May Be Harder to Detect

Several factors complicate the analysis of AI's impact on entry-level jobs. New graduates often enter occupations that are somewhat protected from immediate automation because they involve a mix of tasks, some of which require human judgment or relationship management that AI cannot fully replicate.

Entry-level positions also serve as training grounds where workers develop skills by performing routine supporting tasks under supervision. If AI automates those supporting tasks, new graduates may face a longer onboarding period as they learn through alternative pathways, rather than immediate job loss.

The CESifo researchers note that firms have yet to report large-scale task replacement through AI in Census Bureau surveys, even as adoption accelerates. This suggests the displacement effects, if they exist, may not yet be visible in aggregate employment statistics.

---

## Warnings Against Complacency

The researchers are careful to distinguish between current trends and future outcomes. The absence of significant displacement in summer 2026 data does not imply that AI's labor market impact will remain benign. Firms may be in a transitional period where they are still integrating AI tools without having yet restructured job roles.

Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen has argued that AI only became capable enough to perform meaningful cognitive tasks as of late 2025. If that assessment is correct, the labor market effects of current-generation AI tools may not yet be visible in employment data that reflects hiring decisions made before widespread deployment.

The researchers recommend continued monitoring as AI capabilities advance and firms adjust their workforce planning accordingly.
