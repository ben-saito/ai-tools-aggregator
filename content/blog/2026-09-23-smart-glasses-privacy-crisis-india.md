# Smart Glasses Spark Privacy Crisis in India as Abuse Cases Multiply

A graphic designer in Delhi was packing for a move when a friend sent her an Instagram video. The footage, recorded days earlier at a protest against a bill that would have narrowed legal recognition for transgender people in India, showed the designer being confronted by a content creator known for rage-bait videos. The creator had asked why she was wearing "female clothing." She had told him to back off. Only when the video went viral did she learn he had recorded the exchange using Meta smart glasses styled to look like ordinary Wayfarers.

The edited video drew millions of views, along with transphobic abuse, reaction videos, and AI-generated memes. It spread from Instagram to X and YouTube. Copies remain online today.

This incident, reported by MIT Technology Review, illustrates a growing problem in India as smart glasses from Meta, Reliance Jio, and other manufacturers proliferate in one of the world's fastest-growing wearables markets.

---

## The Privacy Problem

Smart glasses present a fundamental privacy challenge that existing devices do not: they look like fashion accessories while housing high-quality cameras capable of inconspicuous recording. Unlike a smartphone, which most people recognize as a recording device, smart glasses can capture footage without the subject's awareness or consent.

"The core privacy issue is that these cameras don't look like cameras, so targets may not notice they're being recorded," said Janusz Swierczynski, an Oxford Said Business School researcher who studies privacy and emerging technology.

Yet manufacturers have shown limited willingness to take responsibility for how their devices are used. Meta has rejected the notion that it bears responsibility for protecting people from intrusions enabled by its glasses, according to Swierczynski. Instead, the company places the burden on bystanders to spot a small LED that activates when recording is in progress.

That expectation is increasingly untenable. Tutorials available online demonstrate how to cover or disable the recording light on Meta glasses, and a market for "stealth mode" modifications has emerged. Meanwhile, Reliance Jio is preparing to launch a sub-$105 competitor to Meta's $420 glasses later this year.

---

## Disproportionate Harm

The risks associated with covert recording via smart glasses are not distributed equally. Women, transgender individuals, and other marginalized groups face greater harm when inconspicuous devices capture and circulate footage without consent.

"Women and other marginalized groups face greater harm when inconspicuous devices capture and circulate footage without consent," Swierczynski said.

The help line Meri Trustline, which works with Meta and YouTube to support people harmed by online content, has seen an uptick in cases involving smart glasses recordings. Cases include people recorded in medical settings, during private encounters, and in public spaces. Those with less power to object are particularly vulnerable.

"It is either trolling or it is playing pranks on them and then recording them," said a counselor at Meri Trustline. "Abusers do target people who are a little more unaware or susceptible to how this technology-facilitated harm works."

---

## Police Adoption Compounds the Problem

Smart glasses adoption in India extends beyond consumer devices to law enforcement. In June 2026, when thousands of young demonstrators gathered to protest problems with the country's education system, Delhi police used Meta smart glasses to record them. Former Jawaharlal Nehru University Students' Union president Aishe Ghosh filed a petition before the Delhi High Court alleging police use of the devices.

This dynamic complicates the privacy debate in India, where authorities are simultaneously deploying AI-powered surveillance tools including facial recognition systems and smart glasses, while also failing to protect citizens from misuse of those same technologies.

"India's laws against voyeurism and the sharing of intimate images were designed largely around more recognizable forms of abuse," said Apar Gupta, a lawyer and founder-director of the Internet Freedom Foundation.

Under India's current rules, platforms can have up to 36 hours to act on certain content removal complaints. That window is often long enough for a video to be downloaded, copied, and redistributed.

---

## The Regulatory Vacuum

India lacks specific legislation addressing the risks posed by smart glasses. Privacy law in the country has not kept pace with the rapid adoption of wearable cameras and AI-powered image analysis. Experts warn that enforcement gaps are particularly acute given that law enforcement agencies themselves are among the adopters of the technology.

"The hardware is improving, but the country's weak understanding of consent gives an invisible camera far more room to cause harm," said Sameer Parmar, a counselor at Meri Trustline. "Simply being in a public place is often treated as implicit consent to be photographed or filmed."

The problem appears set to grow. According to the Financial Times, internal prototypes of new Meta glasses include AI features that continuously photograph and listen to the wearer's surroundings, enabling the glasses to later recall what they saw or heard. Meta has said it intends to keep the LED for active capture such as photo and video recording, but not for AI features that interpret the wearer's surroundings. The argument is that a constantly blinking light could become so commonplace that people stop noticing it.

That logic risks putting society in a perpetual game of catch-up, said Woodrow Hartzog, a professor of law at Boston University.

"Tech companies have an incredible ability to push these tools out faster than society can meaningfully acclimate to the threat," he said. "By the time society catches up, the harm has already been done."

For the Delhi graphic designer, the harm came quickly. "Being comprehended along the lines of a narrative that I didn't compose or consent to -- that imposed recognition makes you feel like you're powerless in how people know you," she told MIT Technology Review.
