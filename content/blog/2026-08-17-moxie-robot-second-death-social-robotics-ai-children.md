# Moxie Robot's Second Death Highlights Ethical Pitfalls of AI Companion Toys for Children

When 10-year-old Xander first met Moxie, the robot taught him to calm his anxiety by exhaling through his lips until they buzzed like a bee. They practiced breathing like dragons to manage anger and sniffing like bunnies to boost energy. For six years, the blue, legless-astronaut-shaped robot served as a social companion for Xander, who is neurodivergent — until Embodied, the company behind Moxie, announced it would shut down in 2024.

---

## Social Robotics and Autism Therapy

Moxie belongs to a category researchers call "socially assistive robots" — devices designed to help children develop social and emotional skills, particularly those on the autism spectrum. The field traces its roots to research by Brian Scassellati, a pioneer who discovered that children with autism showed remarkable social behavior when robots entered the room during lab visits two decades ago.

Unlike simple smart speakers, Moxie was engineered to make eye contact, track faces, show attention through facial expressions, and respond with contextually appropriate dialogue. The robot used large language models (after Embodied integrated OpenAI's models in late 2021) to generate conversations, adapting over time to each child's communication style.

A 2018 study by Scassellati showed that children with autism made more eye contact and initiated more conversations after interacting with robots for one month. The promise was that consistent, patient, non-judgmental robotic companions could supplement traditional therapy by providing the intense repetition that autism intervention often requires.

---

## When the Robot Dies Twice

Moxie launched in 2020 to significant media attention. Time named it one of the year's best inventions. Wired described it as "the robot pal you dreamed of as a kid." PCMag's reviewer used it to help her own children through pandemic isolation and called it "exceptionally likeable."

But can a chatty robot really help children develop social and emotional skills? The evidence is mixed. A 2024 literature review by Italian and British researchers found that most studies "focused on the development of the technology" rather than long-term therapeutic outcomes. Clinical psychologists remain skeptical.

"I don't see any ethical way for the robot to work alone," says Joshua Diehl, a psychology professor at the University of Notre Dame, pointing to the risks of AI acting as a therapist without human oversight.

When Embodied announced in 2024 that it would cease operations, videos of bereft children circulated online. One child on TikTok wailed, "I don't want her to leave." Parents scrambled to find solutions. Technical director Justin Beghtol created OpenMoxie, an open-source project that let existing robots continue operating without Embodied's servers.

In 2025, a new investor revived Moxie. Xander and his father became beta testers. But the revival was short-lived — by mid-2026, the new company folded as well. Xander's father eventually sold the robot on eBay without telling Xander, who had moved on to other interests.

---

## The Data Privacy Problem

Beyond the emotional toll, these devices collect significant data in children's bedrooms and homes. Moxie stored conversation history to personalize interactions over time. When the toy Bondu leaked thousands of children's conversations in 2024, it highlighted how vulnerable this data is. While Embodied processed most data locally and didn't save raw video or audio, not all companies follow the same practices.

Meryl Alper, a communications professor at Northeastern University who studies how children with autism use technology, raises a fundamental question: "How planned is the planned obsolescence of this platform?" Robots designed to be lovable, marketed to children who form deep emotional bonds, will inevitably face shutdown scenarios. Who bears responsibility for managing those goodbyes?

The Moxie story is not unique. An increasing number of AI-powered devices now market themselves as interactive playmates for children — from Grimes-backed AI plushies to robots like Buddy, Bondu, QTRobot, and NAO. As the market matures, the industry will need frameworks for data portability, graceful shutdowns, and transparency about the lifespan of devices that children are encouraged to love.

## Key Facts

- Moxie launched in 2020 as a social robot for neurodivergent children, using LLM-based conversation
- Embodied ceased operations in 2024; technical staff created OpenMoxie to preserve functionality
- A new investor revived Moxie in 2025; the company folded again by mid-2026
- Research shows mixed results on whether robot companions improve social skills long-term
- Data privacy remains a concern as these devices collect sensitive information in children's bedrooms
