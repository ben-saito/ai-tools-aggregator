# How Kids Feel About AI: MIT Technology Review’s Survey

MIT Technology Review published findings from conversations with children about artificial intelligence, revealing unexpected nuances in how young people perceive and interact with AI systems. The research, conducted as part of MIT’s ongoing study of youth and technology, found that children hold sophisticated but often contradictory views about AI’s role in their lives.

Rather than viewing AI as purely beneficial or harmful, children in the study expressed context-dependent attitudes. They distinguished between AI used for education, entertainment, and surveillance with surprising subtlety. Many children were aware of AI’s limitations and biases but had limited understanding of how AI systems actually work under the hood.

The research highlights a gap between how adults frame AI risks and how young users actually experience the technology. Children reported using AI regularly for homework help, creative projects, and entertainment, but expressed concerns about privacy, job displacement, and the authenticity of AI-generated content.

“When we set out to talk to kids about artificial intelligence, we thought we knew what we’d hear,” the researchers noted. “We expected some to be enthusiastic and others skeptical. What surprised us was how differentiated their views were across different use cases.”

---

## Key Findings

Children in the study showed awareness of AI capabilities that surprised researchers, including knowledge of how AI training data can embed biases. Several children mentioned specific instances where they believed AI systems had treated them unfairly, particularly in educational contexts where AI proctoring tools are deployed.

The youngest participants showed less concern about long-term AI risks, focusing more on immediate usability and privacy implications. Older children, particularly teenagers, expressed more anxiety about AI’s impact on future employment and social dynamics.

Most children interviewed had used generative AI tools personally, either through school resources or personal devices. Their usage patterns varied widely, from creative writing assistance to coding help and research support.

---

## Implications for AI Education

The findings suggest current AI literacy efforts may not align with how children actually think about the technology. Researchers recommend educational approaches that engage with children’s existing concerns rather than imposing adult-centric risk frameworks.

Schools deploying AI tools should consider student perspectives when selecting and implementing these systems, the report suggests. Children’s willingness to critique AI systems may be a resource for identifying problems that adult-centric evaluations miss.
