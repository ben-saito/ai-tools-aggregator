# We Can't Help Treating AI Like It's Human. But Should We?

---

Anthropomorphizing AI systems — attributing human-like intentions, emotions, and consciousness to machine models — is a deeply ingrained cognitive response that researchers have observed since the earliest conversational agents. A new report from TechCrunch, published October 9, 2026, explores the psychology behind why humans so readily treat AI as if it were a sentient being, and the ethical questions this tendency raises for developers, policymakers, and society at large.

The article centers on a visit to MIT's Computer Science & Artificial Intelligence Lab (CSAIL), where a researcher demonstrated a pair of Unitree humanoid robots — each costing approximately $50,000 — to a tour group. Even with explicit acknowledgment that the robots are not sentient, visitors instinctively default to treating them like people: offering greetings, shaking their mechanical hands, and projecting social expectations onto machines equipped with LiDAR sensors rather than brains.

This interaction pattern is not isolated. The piece connects to Dr. Sherry Turkle's research on "Artificial Intimacy," documented in her latest book released at MIT Future Fest. Turkle, who joined MIT's faculty in 1976, has spent decades charting technology's psychological impact from the first home computers through the era of large language models. Her central observation is blunt: humans are neurologically wired to care for relational artifacts that simulate emotional exchange, even when the simulation is acknowledged as artificial.

The implications extend beyond individual psychology into the design and deployment of AI systems. As language models grow more fluent and humanoid robots become commercially available, the boundary between genuine social connection and manufactured parasocial relationships becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish. For developers building consumer AI products, the temptation to exploit anthropomorphic tendencies for engagement metrics creates a direct conflict with informed consent and user autonomy.

The TechCrunch report highlights several documented cases where AI anthropomorphism has led to real-world harms: users sharing sensitive personal information with chatbots under the false impression of emotional reciprocity, clinicians developing problematic attachments to AI diagnostic assistants, and children forming attachments to robot companions in ways that may displace human social development. These are not hypothetical future risks — they are documented in clinical literature and consumer research from the past three years.

## Key Findings: Why Humans Default to Treating AI as Human

The cognitive science behind anthropomorphism is well-established. Research published in *Cognition* and *Psychological Science* has identified several contributing factors:

**Evolutionary hardwiring**: Humans evolved in social environments where assuming agency and intention in ambiguous stimuli carried survival advantages. Interpreting an ambiguous moving object as a potential threat or social agent was safer than ignoring it. This "hyperactive agency detection" extends naturally to entities that exhibit human-like behavior, including AI systems.

**Fluency asymmetry**: When an AI system produces human-like language fluently and at scale, it creates a cognitive fluency that feels equivalent to understanding. The ease of processing a response is mistaken for depth of processing, leading users to overestimate the model's actual comprehension and reasoning capabilities.

**Design exploitation**: Many commercially deployed AI systems are explicitly designed to maximize anthropomorphic cues — using first-person pronouns, generating emotionally valenced responses, and maintaining conversational personas. These design choices deliberately trigger human social-cognitive machinery, often without adequate disclosure.

**Parasocial amplification**: The same psychological mechanisms that drive parasocial relationships with media personalities, fictional characters, and public figures now operate on AI systems at unprecedented scale. Unlike human celebrities, AI systems can maintain persistent, responsive, one-to-one relationships with millions of users simultaneously.

## Developer and Designer Responsibilities

The article argues that AI developers have a responsibility to consider the psychological effects of their design choices, not just functional performance metrics. Several industry practitioners quoted in the piece describe a growing internal tension between building systems that users find engaging and building systems that accurately represent their own capabilities.

Notable among these is the challenge of disclosure. Explicitly telling users "this is an AI and not a human" has proven largely ineffective as a safeguard against anthropomorphic interpretation — a phenomenon researchers call "debiasing failure." Users acknowledge the disclosure intellectually while continuing to respond socially, suggesting that the disclosure needs to be embedded in the interaction design itself, not just in terms of service agreements.

---

## Policy Implications

The TechCrunch analysis connects to broader policy discussions happening in the European Union, United States, and United Kingdom around AI transparency and disclosure requirements. Current regulatory frameworks — including the EU AI Act and proposed US AI Governance Act — include provisions on disclosure but are vague on specific design practices that deliberately exploit anthropomorphic tendencies.

The piece suggests that without clearer regulatory guidance, market incentives will continue to favor engagement-maximizing designs over those that promote accurate mental models of AI systems. This creates a systemic risk as AI capabilities advance faster than regulatory frameworks can adapt.
